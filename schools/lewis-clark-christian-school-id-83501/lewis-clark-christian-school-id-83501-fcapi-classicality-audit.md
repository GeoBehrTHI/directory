## Executive Summary

Lewis Clark Christian School (Lewiston, ID) presents a robust Christian, classical-leaning program that emphasizes formation, virtue, and a biblically grounded worldview. Public materials consistently tie education to discipleship, community, and a biblical framework for life, with explicit theology-forming components such as a Statement of Faith, integration of Bible and theology into humanities, and a mission centered on godly character and family partnership. The curriculum shows clear classical markers—Latin study, rhetoric, a history-focused humanities approach, and a two-hour humanities block intended to weave worldview across disciplines. There are strong signs of communal practices (fellowship, family involvement, mentoring across grades) and a living-teacher/mentorship ethos, including explicit mention of older students mentoring younger ones.

Overall, the school aligns with many core classical elements, particularly in religious/metaphysical foundations, formation-focused aims, and a curriculum enriched with the humanities, rhetoric, and Latin. Some aspects that typically accompany a fully classical model—open advocacy for Great Books, explicit Socratic pedagogy in upper grades, and a fully articulated “living curriculum” faculty culture—are less evident in public-facing content. Taken together, the school is best characterized as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL, with strong classical fingerprints and clear Christian formation, but with room to more explicitly demonstrate certain classical-methodological practices (notably explicit Socratic pedagogy and a broader Great-Books orientation) in its public materials.

What would move the needle? 
- More explicit articulation and evidence of Socratic/seminar-based pedagogy in upper grades (with concrete classroom practices). 
- A clearer public-facing commitment to Great Books/Great Conversation and the Great-Books canon, including representative titles and a visible curricular map. 
- A more explicit portrayal of the faculty as a “living curriculum” with teacher portraits, continuous mentorship, and ongoing faculty collaboration as a form of curriculum. 
- Expanded parent-education offerings that frame parents as co-educators with structured opportunities (book studies, seminars) and transparent pathways for parent formation aligned with classical aims.

Positive indicators include a Statement of Faith, Bible/theology integrated into humanities, and mission language centered on discipleship and biblical community. No clear negative indicators are present in the public materials we examined.
  Positive keyword indicators observed: theology, doctrine, creed, biblical worldview, discipleship, the Christian worldview permeating school life, integration of faith and reason.
  Why this rating: The school grounds its entire program in Christian faith and theology, with explicit doctrinal statements and faith-based formation evident across multiple pages. This aligns strongly with the religious/metaphysical foundations expected in a classical framework.

- Element 2: Purpose and Goal — CLASSICAL
  Evidence points to formation, virtue, and wisdom as aims rather than mere information or workforce preparation. The mission emphasizes loving God and neighbor, discipleship, and moral formation; a transcendent view of truth, goodness, and beauty is implicit in the biblical framing and the call for biblical formation of character.
  Positive keyword indicators observed: formation, virtue, moral imagination, human flourishing, love of learning, telos, pursuit of the true/good/beautiful, biblical worldview, formation of affections.
  Why this rating: The school’s stated aims center on formation rather than information, with virtues and a Christian telos emphasized throughout, matching classical expectations for purpose and goal.

- Element 3: Curriculum — CLASSICAL
  Evidence shows a curriculum that foregrounds liberal arts sensibilities (grammar/Latin, rhetoric, and a humanities-centric approach) and integrated study across disciplines (Bible, literature, history, geography, theology). The program includes Latin study, rhetoric within diploma tracks, and a two-hour humanities block that deliberately weaves worldview across subjects. Evidence of a history-forward, literature-rich approach and a visible emphasis on classical subjects is present; explicit Great-Books mention is not clearly shown in public content, but Latin, rhetoric, and integrated humanities are clearly highlighted.
  Positive keyword indicators observed: grammar/Latin, rhetoric, history, literature, theology, scripture, great books (explicitly referenced in some classical-discourse materials, and strongly suggested via “great conversation” language in classical-canon lexicon; not always explicit on the site), two-hour humanities block, integrated humanities, core knowledge language in early grades.
  Why this rating: The curriculum demonstrates core classical elements (Latin, rhetoric, integrated humanities, historical-literary integration) and a clearly stated aim of forming the whole person through a classical liberal-arts approach, placing it solidly in the CLASSICAL spectrum though with room to more explicitly foreground Great Books canon and a full “Great Conversation” framing.

- Element 4: Pedagogy — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
  Evidence includes a strong emphasis on integrated humanities and a central humanities block; rhetoric and writing appear in diploma planning; a blend of contemplative/restful pedagogy (implied by the emphasis on formation, wonder, and memory-work) in contrast to a purely project-based or purely faculty-led approach.
  Positive keyword indicators observed: contemplative learning, scholé (restful learning), wonder, memory work, recitation, rhetoric, seminars (explicit “Socratic discussions” are not consistently evidenced in public content; the site emphasizes a two-hour humanities block and rhetoric in the diploma track).
  Negative keyword indicators observed: explicit, universal evidence of Socratic seminars in ordinary pedagogy is not consistently visible; the site does not uniformly present a full, canonical Socratic-programme across all upper grades.
  Why this rating: The pedagogy leans classical with memory and rhetoric components and a contemplative strand, but it does not consistently demonstrate the full spectrum of classical pedagogy (notably explicit, widespread Socratic seminars across all grades in public materials). This yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rating.

- Element 5: Faculty — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
  Evidence shows a community emphasis that hints at mentorship and living-curriculum principles (older students mentoring younger; a culture of virtue and Christian formation). Public statements emphasize a collaborative, community-oriented ethos rather than a fully documented formal “faculty as living curriculum” portrait.
  Positive keyword indicators observed: teacher as mentor, living curriculum (phrases such as discipling, mentorship by older students, communal life), collegial culture inferred through family involvement and shared mission.
  Negative keyword indicators observed: explicit portraits or formalized “faculty as living curriculum” posts, and a catalog of faculty virtues/character profiles are not clearly present in the public content.
  Why this rating: The school reflects a strong mentorship/community ethos consistent with classical aims, but the public-facing materials do not systematically present a formal “faculty portrait” or a living-curriculum model in the way some classical schools do. This yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL designation.

- Element 6: Parents & Community — CLASSICAL
  Evidence shows clear partnership with families and a biblically grounded formation emphasis. The mission highlights assisting parents in discipling children, a Christian worldview permeating school life, and family involvement as a core cultural practice. The program also indicates openness to parent engagement (through newsletters, public-facing information, and an emphasis on parent involvement in the school culture).
  Positive keyword indicators observed: partnership with families, parental formation or education, family involvement, open campus or parent-access to the school, parent-education language around classical philosophy (as evidenced by the mission and community language).
  Negative keyword indicators observed: the public materials do not present a heavy consumer-centric model (no strong “parents as customers” language). The content does not show a formal, wide-aperture program for ongoing parent-book studies or parent-co-teaching in a well-documented way, though some formation-oriented parent engagement is described.
  Why this rating: The school emphasizes parent partnership and family involvement in formation and community life, aligning well with classical expectations for parental partnership and shared mission.

Aggregate Ranking: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Rationale: The school exhibits strong classical markers in religion, formation, curriculum, and parent/community alignment, with solid evidence of Latin, rhetoric, integrated humanities, and a biblically grounded mission. However, the public materials are less explicit about full Socratic pedagogy across all grades and the canonical Great Books/Great Conversation canon, which prevents a full CLASSICAL verdict. The overall profile aligns with SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL, reflecting meaningful classical elements plus opportunities to make certain classical-methodological aspects more explicit.

What Would Move the Needle

- Pedagogy (Element 4): Publish and demonstrate explicit Socratic/seminar-based practices across upper grades, including sample lesson plans or classroom vignettes showing Socratic dialogue, questions, and debate formats. Promote scholé days and contemplative sequences with concrete schedules and outcomes.
- Curriculum (Element 3): Include and publicly map a Great Books/Great Conversation component, with representative titles and cross-curricular integration, to strengthen the Great Books canon alignment and the Trivium emphasis beyond rhetoric and Latin alone.
- Faculty (Element 5): Publish a visible “portrait of the teacher” and a documented living-curriculum approach, including faculty development focused on classical pedagogy, and profiles of teachers modeling virtue and scholarship. Establish a formal, ongoing faculty collaboration or seminar culture as part of the public-facing curriculum narrative.
- Parents & Community (Element 6): Expand explicit parent-formation offerings (e.g., book studies, lectures, and seminars) that align with classical criteria and demonstrate active parental co-educator roles. Provide clear, scheduled opportunities for parent visits, learning communities, and involvement in school life beyond events.
