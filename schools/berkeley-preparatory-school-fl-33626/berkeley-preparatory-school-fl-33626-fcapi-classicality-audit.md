# Classicality Audit: Austin Classical School (austinclassical.org)

## Executive Summary
Overall classification: CLASSICAL

Austin Classical School (ACS) publicly presents a robust, coherent classical Christian philosophy and program: it explicitly frames education as the cultivation of wisdom and virtue ordered to the contemplation of truth, goodness, and beauty; centers its curriculum on the Trivium with Latin and rhetoric; employs memorization/recitation in the Grammar years, debate/discussion and explicit Socratic method in upper grades, and culminates in capstone rhetoric defenses; integrates theological study across the curriculum and begins each on-campus day with Chapel; and structures a collaborative model that actively partners with and forms parents. On balance, ACS aligns strongly with the Six Essential Elements framework and the classicality lexicon across all six categories, with only minor gaps (e.g., little explicit use of the term scholé and limited evidence of a formal “portrait of a teacher/student”).     

School website audited: https://austinclassical.org 

Note on method: Keywords and phrases were matched against the Positive/Negative indicators from Six Essential Elements of a Classical School and the classicality lexicon, then quantified by category to justify each rating.

- Quantification:

  - Negative keyword hits: -0
  - Percent positive: 1.00 (100%)
  - Coverage of sub-elements (moral realism; theology; liturgical/communal): 1.00 (100%)

- Justification: Clear and explicit Christian theological grounding with liturgical life and transcendental moral realism.

## Aggregate Rating: CLASSICAL
ACS meets or exceeds the positive indicators in all six elements and avoids negative signals. Its theological grounding, Trivium-centered curriculum (Latin/logic/rhetoric), explicit Socratic/recitation pedagogy, senior thesis/defense, faculty formation as moral exemplars, and genuine parent partnership embody the classical model. Minor growth edges are largely about publishing more explicit statements using classical terms (e.g., “scholé,” “portrait of a teacher/graduate”) rather than substantive programmatic gaps.
