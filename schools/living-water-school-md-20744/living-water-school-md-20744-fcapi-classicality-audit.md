## Executive Summary

The Living Water School MD 20744 presents a robust Classical education orientation integrated with a distinctive Sudbury-inspired, student-led element. Public-facing materials consistently emphasize classical elements (Trivium, Latin, logic, rhetoric, Great Books/Living Books, Socratic seminars) alongside a Christ-centered, Biblical worldview, and deliberate communal practices (worship, Bible study, family partnership). While the school highlights traditional classical aims—virtue, wisdom, Great Books, liberal arts, and Socratic pedagogy—it also foregrounds a democratic, student-led dimension (Sudbury model) that introduces some non-classical practice into the learning environment. This combination yields a generally Classical profile with notable non-classical threads.

Overall classification: CLASSICAL.

Key drivers of the assessment:
- Strong classical curriculum signals (Grammar/Logic/Rhetoric, Latin, logic, rhetoric, classical literature, Great Books/Classic literature references) and explicit mention of Socratic seminars and rhetorical training.
- Clear religious/metaphysical foundations (Christ-centered, Biblical worldview, worship, Bible study) that align with classical criteria.
- Explicit attention to formation of character, virtue, and the “whole person” through a philosophically classical framing, even as the school accommodates a democratic, student-led component.
- Evidence of a formal accreditation framework (Middle States) and a structured, age-phase Trivium model (Little Springs, Creeks, Rivers, Bays) to organize curriculum and pedagogy.

Notes on needle-moving considerations:
- The integration of a Sudbury-inspired, student-led model introduces non-classical pedagogy signals (per the classical lexicon), which places Element 2 and Element 4 nuances toward the middle of the classical spectrum. However, the weight of explicit classical curriculum, pedagogy, and community practices remains strong enough to maintain an overall CLASSICAL designation.

- There is explicit emphasis on Liberal Arts Education within a Classical framework, including engagement with Living Books/Classic literature as the curricular backbone and a strong emphasis on primary sources/classics (Great Books/Classic literature). The language around “Living Books/Classic literature” and the classical education framing appears in multiple pages, including the “The Living Water School Way” and philosophy posts , .
- History is treated as history within the curriculum rather than as “social studies,” aligning with a classical approach that foregrounds Western Civilization and historical study within core disciplines .
- The school references Socratic seminars, logic, rhetoric, and a commitment to reading thick texts (Aristotle, Homer, etc.) through the Living Books approach (the language emphasizes classical pedagogy and living literature) .
- Latin and other classical languages are part of the curriculum in the classical frame described by the school (e.g., Latin in the semestral offerings and core subjects) .

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: liberal arts education, Trivium (grammar/logic/rhetoric), Great Books/Classic literature, Latin, history as Western civilization, Socratic seminars, memorization/narration/recitation (narration/recitation appear in the lexicon and are consistent with classical pedagogy and memory work), and “memory work” and “chanting” (presence of practice that aligns with traditional pedagogy).

This is a restorative/classical signal rather than a negative.
- Quantitative note: Positive keywords (grammar, logic, rhetoric, Great Books/Classic literature, history as content, Latin, Socratic seminars) predominate; explicit counter-signals from the lexicon are not evident in the materials.

Why this rating
- The curriculum aligns closely with classical guidelines: Trivium structure, liberal arts, engagement with classic literature, and Socratic pedagogy. The explicit historical framing (not as “social studies”) further supports a Classical orientation.

Citations: Trivium and four-phase structure are described in the site’s public materials about the trivium and stages (Little Springs, Creeks, Rivers, Bays) ; Liberal Arts/Classical Education language and “Living Books/Classic literature” are described in the philosophy/education pages ; history framing as not mere “social studies” is noted in the curriculum description ; Latin and other classical language emphasis is reflected in the public content around the Trivium and classical pedagogy .

### Element 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The school explicitly describes Socratic discussion in upper grades and the development of logic and rhetoric, with opportunities to give speeches and participate in rhetorical exercises in higher grades. Public materials describe a day where classical learning is complemented by student-driven exploration, including periods for Socratic seminars and practice in rhetoric, and an emphasis on memory, recitation, and formal oratory .
- The lower grades emphasize a contemplative/restful dimension and activities that deepen memory (e.g., singing, chanting, and memory-reinforcing activities) in line with scholé pedagogy, even if not always named as such in the source; this aligns with the classical pedagogy of contemplative learning and memory work .
- The philosophy describes a half-day structure where classical learning is interwoven with freedom to pursue personal interests; in effect, a mix of rigorous classical practices with time for contemplation and autonomous exploration. This setup emulates the scholé approach (restful learning, contemplation) in balance with Socratic inquiry .
- The school’s public materials indicate upper-level engagement in Socratic seminars, debates, and rhetoric training, along with opportunities to present and develop speeches, which is a hallmark of classical pedagogy .

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: Socratic seminars, seminars, dialectic, rhetoric, oratory, debate, memory work, narrations, chanting, scholé, contemplation, wonder, seminars.
- Negative keywords present: The Sudbury model’s student-led, open-democracy aspects could be interpreted as non-classical pedagogy signals; however, the public materials emphasize a balanced approach with substantial classical pedagogy (lower/upper school differences). The explicit negative signals from the lexicon (e.g., purely project-based or student-directed pedagogy) are not primary in the public materials.
- Quantitative note: Strong presence of classical pedagogy keywords (Socratic, rhetoric, memory, narration, discourse) with limited explicit negative signals; thus category leans Classical.

Why this rating
- The pedagogy is anchored in classical methods (Socratic seminars, rhetoric, memory work, living literature) and supports a robust classical teaching approach, with additional restorative/restful elements. The presence of a Sudbury-inspired, more student-led component introduces non-classical signals, but the dominant pedagogy described remains classical.

Citations: Socratic seminars and rhetoric/explicit classical pedagogy are described in the Launch/philosophy content (Socratic seminar, rhetoric, speeches) ; the lower- and upper-school pedagogy balance (memory, chanting, rest in scholé) is described in the broader philosophy references and the nature of the day structure ; the description of the mixed day structure is detailed in the same sources .

What Would Move the Needle

- If aiming to move Element 2 from SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL to CLASSICAL, the school could more explicitly foreground formation as the sole central goal of education, with a reduction (or clearer separation) of job-readiness/workforce-preparation signals in official mission statements, and a stronger articulation of virtue/wisdom as primary telos (beyond life-after-high-school planning). This would reduce modern workforce-oriented language and strengthen formation-centered language in the public-facing materials.

- Concrete steps could include: explicitly framing Pathways and internships as complementary to formation without treating them as primary telos; highlighting virtue, wisdom, and the permanent questions as the primary aims in the school’s mission statements; ensuring all public content links to scholé/restful learning as a central pedagogy, not only as a supplementary approach.

Citations for needle-moving considerations
- Pathways/college-vocational planning and job-readiness emphasis are present in the Bays section and Pathways description, illustrating a workforce preparation current .
- Formation-focused language and “whole person” framing in the public materials signal the classical telos and could be foregrounded more strongly to move toward a more unified classical messaging .
