## Executive Summary

Christ Classical Academy of Charlotte (CCAC) presents a clearly classical, Christian educational program. Public materials from the school emphasize Bible-centered, theologically grounded formation within a classical framework, a mission to cultivate virtue and wisdom, and a curriculum rooted in literature, writing, and critical thinking. The school’s statements repeatedly connect education with formation (not merely information) and with the pursuit of truth, goodness, and beauty. Across categories, CCAC demonstrates strong alignment with classical ideals, especially in religious/metaphysical foundations, school purpose/goals, and curriculum, with consistent signals in pedagogy and community partnerships. Some elements of pedagogy and faculty presentation are less explicit in public materials, which makes Element 4 (Pedagogy) and Element 5 (Faculty) somewhat less fully classical than the other categories, though still broadly aligned. The aggregate assessment places CCAC in CLASSICAL territory.

Key insights:
- Religious/metaphysical foundations are explicit and central: Bible integration, chapel, and explicit theological commitments anchor the school’s approach (Evidence: site descriptions of Bible classes, weekly chapel, and a philosophy emphasizing biblical truth).  
- The school’s aim is formation through a classical framework: mission statements and philosophy stress formation, virtue, and the truth/goodness/beauty triad within a classical curriculum. 
- Curriculum emphasizes literature, writing, and critical thinking within a classical milieu; explicit Great Books language appears in the broader classical education literature the school aligns with. Public materials describe a literature-and-critical-thinking emphasis and a classically designed curriculum. 
- Pedagogy signals include conversation-based learning and smaller classes; explicit Socratic/seminar practices are not always named, but the classical approach and memory-based routines are indicated. 
- Parents and community are framed as partners; philosophy statements highlight partnerships with families in education.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - The CCAC materials emphasize smaller class sizes and conversation-based learning, and note a classically designed curriculum that supports memory and dialogue. Public descriptions mention conversation-based learning and a focus on close student-teacher connections. 
  - While explicit naming of Socratic seminars or the full set of classical pedagogical methods (recitation, memorization, debate, etc.) is not consistently stated in public materials, the classical approach and opportunities for discussion are foregrounded. 
- Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
  - Positive keywords present: conversation-based learning, restfulness/contemplation (implied by the classical pedagogy), memorization/recitation (not explicitly named but supported by the classical approach terminology in broader canon), small class size, teacher-student dialogue.
  - Negative keywords: none overtly present in public materials.
  - The lexicon for Pedagogy lists items like Socratic seminar, narration, chanting, restfulness (scholé), and debate as standard classical methods. CCAC shows some of these ideas (conversation-based learning, small classes) but does not consistently name all canonical elements (e.g., explicit Socratic seminars or extensive recitation). This partial alignment justifies SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than FULL CLASSICAL.
- Why this rating
  - CCAC demonstrates clear classical bent in pedagogy (small classes, discussion-oriented learning) but lacks explicit, consistent articulation of all canonical classical methods (e.g., named Socratic seminars, routine memorization/narration in public materials). This yields a category rating of SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

### Element 5: Faculty — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence

However, explicit descriptions of the faculty as the “living curriculum” or as “portrait of a teacher” are not consistently documented in the public-facing materials. 
  - There is evidence of a formal leadership and faculty structure (e.g., Mission/Vision, Faculty/Staff pages) but explicit “living curriculum” language or clear statements about faculty as exemplars of virtue and mentors are not consistently shown in visible content. 
- Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
  - Positive keywords present: mentor/teacher as guide (implied in “trained to teach Christianly,” and “faculty collaborate on topical courses”), living curriculum (not consistently present; where present in classical literature, not clearly reflected in CCAC public copy).
  - Negative keywords: none explicitly present.
  - According to the Six Essential Elements lexicon, classical faculty signals include “portrait of a teacher,” “living curriculum,” and “collegial, virtuous exemplars.” Those specific phrases are not consistently present in the CCAC public materials. This leads to a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL assessment for the Faculty category.
- Why this rating
  - The school presents faculty as aligned with a Christian/classical mission and capable of mentoring students, but the public-facing content does not consistently show the full “living curriculum” or the explicit portrait language that would push this to CLASSICAL. Hence SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

What Would Move the Needle
- For CCAC to move from SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL toward FULL CLASSICAL in Element 4 (Pedagogy) and Element 5 (Faculty):
  - Publish explicit, canonical classical teaching method language (e.g., named Socratic seminars, explicit use of memorization/recitation, narration, and seminar formats) in public materials.
  - Provide explicit statements and anecdotes about the faculty as the living curriculum, including portraits or profiles of teachers illustrating virtue, mastery, and mentorship (e.g., “portrait of a teacher” language, faculty collaboration as core to the curriculum).
  - Expand on the role of the faculty as mentors and exemplars with concrete examples (professional development in classical pedagogy, seminars, and teacher-led classical memory work) in public communications.
  - Increase explicit documentation of parent-co-teaching roles or formalized parent formation activities beyond general partnership language (e.g., classical book studies, liturgical gatherings, structured parent seminars).
  - Strengthen explicit articulation of Great Books or primary texts in the public curriculum descriptions, including examples of approved Great Books lists, to reinforce the Element 3 criterion.
