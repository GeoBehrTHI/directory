## Executive Summary

Generations Christian Academy (GCA) in Franklin, Tennessee presents a distinctly Christian approach to education, with a clear emphasis on biblical worldview, faith-based teaching, and family partnership. Public-facing materials consistently frame education as formation of the whole person—mind, body, and spirit—anchored in scriptural truths, worship, and Christ-centered learning. While the school demonstrates strong religious foundations and a robust parent/community partnership, its curricular and pedagogical language does not explicitly articulate the traditional classical-core components (grammar, logic, rhetoric; Great Books; explicit Socratic pedagogy) that define a classical school in the Six Essential Elements framework. Taken as a whole, GCA is best characterized as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall, with pronounced CLASSICAL facets in religious/metaphysical foundations and parental/community alignment, but lacking explicit emphasis on core classical curriculum and pedagogy hallmarks.

Key insights:

- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations are strongly CLASSICAL: Bible-centered, theology-infused learning, chapel/worship, and a biblical worldview permeate academics and student life. Evidence: Bible as the Word of God; biblical worldview; chapel and prayer events; worship as part of student life; scriptural truths woven into disciplines.
- School Purpose and Goal convey formation and virtue in a Christian frame, supported by formation of the whole student and Christ-centered aims. This category trends CLASSICAL in spirit, though not all classic vocabulary (e.g., explicit “virtue” language) is always named in every page. Positive references to “formation” and “whole student” are present.
- Curriculum and Pedagogy show notable Christian alignment but do not consistently foreground the classic triad of grammar-logic-rhetoric or Great Books. Curriculum emphasizes scriptural truths, biblical worldview, and subject content, but explicit Great Books or liberal-arts sequencing is not stated in the public materials. Pedagogy includes worship, memory work, and public-speaking training, with limited explicit Socratic/seminar-based instruction described publicly. This yields NOT CLASSICAL in some aspects and SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL in others.
- Faculty portrayal centers on faith-guided leadership and a mission-driven staff, with references to leadership by experienced directors and a plainly faith-based hiring ethos. This aligns with classical ideals in spirit but may lack the explicit “living curriculum” portrait language found in some classical programs.
- Parents & Community are explicitly engaged as partners in education, with a family-centered model, resources for parent formation, and welcoming campus visits. This is CLASSICAL in tone and aligns with the classical emphasis on partnership with families.

- Positive keywords present: Bible, Word of God, Biblical worldview, faith-based, Jesus Christ, God, Good News, Scriptural truths, chapel, prayer, worship, ministry, gospel.
- Negative keywords present: None evident in the public materials.
- Ratio (positive vs. negative): High positive, effectively 100% of observed religious/metaphysical indicators are positive within the sampled content.

Why this rating

- The school demonstrates a strong transcendental moral framework and liturgical/community practices (chapel, worship, prayer), aligned with theological grounding. The presence of a biblical worldview and scriptural integration are hallmark indicators for Religious and Metaphysical Foundations in classical education, giving this category a robust CLASSICAL flavor, tempered by the lack of explicit liturgical calendars or sacramental language beyond worship and prayer in the public materials. This yields a category assessed as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than fully CLASSICAL due to broader curricular/pedagogical elements described in other categories.

Citations: biblically grounded statements and worship are described across the site, including the mission and classroom integration (e.g., “We believe the Bible to be the infallible Word of God, and teach our students from a Biblical worldview.”, “The faith-based Christian emphasis and biblical worldview are an integral part of the academic curriculum.”, and “chapel, prayer events, and public speaking training opportunities”).

Overall, Element 2 aligns with CLASSICAL in intent and emphasis.

### Element 3: Curriculum — NOT CLASSICAL

Evidence

- Curriculum materials describe a biblically saturated curriculum and scriptural truths woven into each discipline, with emphasis on reading/writing, mathematical foundations, literature from a biblical viewpoint, and historical/biblical perspectives. They do not explicitly state grammar, logic, rhetoric, Great Books, or a formal Great Conversation.
- The site references “comprehensive curriculum” and standard subject areas, with a focus on biblical worldview and faith integration rather than explicit classical liberal-arts sequencing or classical languages.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: scriptural truths, biblical worldview, biblical standards, Christ-centered, Christian emphasis, faith-based.
- Negative keywords present: Great Books, Great Conversation, grammar, logic, rhetoric, liberal-arts sequencing (not explicitly stated). The absence of these explicit classical elements in the public-facing curriculum content is noticeable.
- Ratio: Positive keywords are present, but explicit classical curriculum keywords (grammar/logic/rhetoric; Great Books; Great Conversation) are not present in the public materials. This yields a NOT CLASSICAL evaluation for Element 3.

Why this rating

- The classical curriculum demands explicit reference to the liberal arts (grammar/logic/rhetoric), Great Books, and the Great Conversation, plus the study of history as a continuous thread rather than a social-studies framing. GCA’s curriculum emphasizes biblical truths and standard subject areas with spiritual integration but does not publicly declare the classical liberal-arts sequence or Great Books approach. Therefore, NOT CLASSICAL for this category.

Citations: Scriptural truths woven through disciplines; biblically saturated curriculum; emphasis on biblical worldview in academics.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence

- Pedagogy features noted include contemplative/restful elements (scholé-like memory emphasis and contemplative learning in some descriptions), and student experiences like worship and public speaking. There are references to memory work and recitation in some classical pedagogy signals (e.g., singing/memorization activities under Lower School contexts). However, the explicit use of Socratic seminars, debates, or graduate-level rhetoric in high school is not publicly described.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: contemplation/scholé concepts (memory, rote/recitation), singing/chanting for memory in lower grades, public speaking training, and opportunities for worship-led learning. These align with traditional pedagogical practices associated with older classical models (memory work, liturgical elements).
- Negative keywords present: absence of explicit Socratic seminars, formal rhetoric training, or heavy emphasis on debate in upper grades in the public content. Constructivist/experiential-learning language is not the focus here (no clear statements endorsing those approaches; the site emphasizes faith-based and teacher-led instruction with smaller class sizes).
- Ratio: Moderate presence of restorative/memory-based pedagogy, with limited explicit Socratic/seminar pedagogy.

Why this rating

- The pedagogy contains elements that echo classical methods (memory work, some contemplative/restful learning) but lacks explicit, widespread use of Socratic seminars and formal rhetoric/debate programs across grades. This places it in SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than fully CLASSICAL.

Citations: references to memory-based learning and contemplative elements; public speaking opportunities; worship and prayer activities as part of student experiences.

Rationale: While GCA excels in Religious and Metaphysical Foundations (Element 1) and demonstrates strong parental/community alignment (Element 6) as well as a clear formation-focused purpose (Element 2), it does not consistently present the core classical curriculum or pedagogy (Element 3 and Element 4) in public materials. The curriculum lacks explicit Great Books, grammar-logic-rhetoric sequencing, and explicit Socratic pedagogy in the accessible content. Pedagogy and faculty show classical echoes but are not fully iconic of classical instruction across grade levels. Taken together, the school leans toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL, with strong classical signals in faith formation and community partnership but lacking full classical curriculum and pedagogy articulation in the public-facing materials.

What Would Move the Needle

- Explicit Great Books/Great Conversation framing: Publish a stated curriculum pathway that identifies Great Books or foundational Western canon texts, including a clearly articulated plan for integrating primary sources and classical reading across grades.
- Core classical pedagogy: Publicly outline the adoption of formal Socratic seminars, Paideia-style seminars, and explicit logic/rhetoric instruction, including grade-level milestones (K–12) for grammar, logic, rhetoric progression.
- Grammar-logic-rhetoric sequence: Add a stated, progressive liberal-arts sequence (grammar, logic, rhetoric) in the curriculum and show examples of student work illustrating these stages.
- Scholé and contemplative pedagogy: Expand and publicly articulate routines for contemplative learning and rest (scholé), including routine memory work, recitation, and restorative classroom practices aligned with classical pedagogy.
- Great Books-based history and literature: Extend history and literature instruction to emphasize classical historical development and primary-source readings (with notes on how they connect to the biblical worldview) as part of a coherent liberal-arts approach.
- Faculty as living curriculum: More explicit statements about the faculty serving as the "living curriculum" with ongoing professional development in classical pedagogy, classical formation, and catechetical methods to reinforce a classical frame.
- Public-facing articulation of formation aims: Strengthen and publish a theory of education (telos) that links virtue formation, wisdom, and love of truth, beauty, and goodness, anchored in the Great Conversation with explicit references to transcendentals and classical virtues.
- Parent formation and home-school synergy: Expand parent-formation programming (book studies, lectures, family-liturgical practices) to deepen the home-school partnership in a way that mirrors classical educational formation.
