## Executive Summary

Wyoming Classical Academy (WCA) presents a clearly articulated classical orientation centered on virtue, moral character, and a liberal-arts based curriculum. Public-facing materials emphasize character formation, civic virtue, and a content-rich classical education built on liberal arts and sciences. The mission explicitly frames education as formation of minds and hearts, not merely information transfer, with aims toward virtuous citizenship. This aligns substantially with classical aims, but the materials reviewed do not prominently assert theology, liturgical practices, or explicit Great Books/Great Conversation rhetoric, and there is limited evidence of canonical classical pedagogy (e.g., Socratic seminars, routine Great Books study) in the public pages. Taken together, the school lands between CLASSICAL and SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL, with the aggregate leaning toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL due to partial gaps in the explicit articulation of core classical practices beyond virtue-centric formation and liberal-arts emphasis.

- Strengths: Clear emphasis on virtue, moral character, civic virtue, and a liberal-arts, content-rich classical framework. Public materials foreground formation of character and a well-rounded liberal arts education (evidence: Vision & Mission and day-to-day descriptions) , .
- Elementary evidence of classical pedagogy or canonical elements is present but not consistently explicit (e.g., no direct public statements of Great Books, daily Socratic seminars, or liturgical practices across the public site) , .
- Parent and community engagement is actively promoted (parent information nights, open enrollment, and visitor access), which aligns with the classical model’s emphasis on parental partnership and communal support , .

Executive category snapshot highlights: Element 2 (School Purpose and Goal) and Element 6 (Parents & Community) are CLASSICAL based on explicit formation aims and strong parent engagement. Categories 1 (Religious and Metaphysical Foundations), 3 (Curriculum), 4 (Pedagogy), and 5 (Faculty) show substantial classical signals but with gaps that keep them at SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL. The aggregate assessment: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

What would move the needle:
- Explicitly articulate and weave in Great Books / Great Conversation language and programs (e.g., mention of Great Books in core history and literature, a formal Great Conversation framework).
- Add explicit articulation of Socratic pedagogy in upper grades and clear, scheduled Socratic seminars/public-facing descriptions.
- Strengthen explicit theology or spiritual formation language (where appropriate for the charter context) and liturgical or communal practices as part of the daily rhythm, if consistent with the school’s identity.
- Provide a public, concrete statement of the living curriculum and teacher-mentorship expectations (the living curriculum, teacher modeling of virtue, and mentorship) to strengthen Element 5.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: virtue, moral character, civic virtue (3 instances aligning with Transcendental Moral Realism and formation of affections).
- Negative keywords present: none clearly evidenced in public materials (no explicit absence claim about theology or liturgy is stated on the site).

The keywords map to a moral-realism orientation (virtue and character) without explicit theological anchoring. This yields SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL, given partial alignment and the absence of explicit liturgical/theological statements in the public content. The primary classical signals (virtue, moral formation) are present, but not the full theological and liturgical scaffolding called out in the rubric.

### Element 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- There is no explicit, public statement describing the use of Socratic seminars, debates, or classical seminars in higher grades, though the claim of a classical education implies some traditional methods may be employed. Public pages do not confirm a consistent use of Socratic pedagogy or formal Great Books/Socratic discussions (which would strengthen the Classical categorization) .

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: eloquence in speaking/writing (rhetoric signals), reading/comprehension, classical education—these map to certain classical pedgagical signals (memory, recitation, language focus) present in the public materials (2–4 signals).
- Negative keywords present: explicit mention of Socratic seminars, formal logic courses, or Great Books-based seminars are not evident in the public materials. The absence of explicit Socratic pedagogy reduces the strength of a full Classical pedagogy label on the site.

Why this rating
- The public content demonstrates pedagogy oriented toward verbal proficiency and core liberal-arts outcomes but lacks explicit documentation of the full range of classical pedagogy (e.g., regular Socratic seminars, rigorous Great Conversations) in the public pages. Thus, SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Citations: Public emphasis on reading/comprehension and eloquence in speaking/writing ; Absence of explicit Socratic pedagogy statements on the public materials .

## What Would Move the Needle

- Explicitly articulate and document a Great Books/Great Conversation framework in curriculum and pedagogy (e.g., scheduled reading lists, core Great Books, and publicly described seminar formats).
- Create and publish clear statements about Socratic pedagogy in the upper grades (e.g., regular Socratic seminars, seminars, and debates with defined assessment methods).
- Publicly emphasize theology or sacred/ethical formation in a way that aligns with the charter’s identity (where appropriate), and include liturgical or liturgical-inspired practices if consistent with the community’s values.
- Clarify and publicize the “living curriculum” concept and faculty mentorship model (teachers as role models, faculty collaboration, and professional formation) to strengthen Element 5.
- Expand explicit references to “grammar, logic, rhetoric” as a formal curricular progression (K-12) to reinforce Element 3.
