## Executive Summary

Valley Christian Schools (VCS) presents a strongly theocentric, formation-oriented approach to education with clear commitments to Biblical worldview, Reformed Christian foundations, and active family partnerships. When weighed against the classical-school rubric, VCS demonstrates robust alignment in Religious and Metaphysical Foundations, School Purpose and Goal, Faculty, and Parents & Community. Its curriculum and pedagogy reflect a traditional, faith-centered approach with explicit emphasis on spiritual formation, character formation, and a community of learners anchored in Christian confession. While it exhibits many classical markers, it does not consistently articulate or implement the core liberal-arts sequence (grammar, logic, rhetoric) or a Great Books-centric curriculum, which depresses it from a full “Classical” rating in Curriculum and, to a lesser extent, in Pedagogy. Overall, the school earns a CLASSICAL classification, with Element 3 (Curriculum) most clearly not fully classical, and Element 4 (Pedagogy) appearing SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than fully Classical.

Key insights at a glance
- Religious & Metaphysical Foundations: Strong, explicit emphasis on Biblical worldview, Reformed tradition, liturgical rhythm (chapels, Bible studies, devotions), and the sacred in everyday learning. This aligns strongly with classical expectations in this dimension. Evidence: Our Beliefs; Spiritual Formation; Who We Are statements describing theology-infused learning, chapel-based practice, and a Coram Deo stance.  
- Purpose & Goal: Education framed as formation—moral, spiritual, intellectual—within a Christian context and partnership with families; emphasis on equipping students for service and lifelong growth rather than mere vocational preparation. This is highly classical in spirit. Evidence: Who We Are; Our Beliefs statements describing formation and partnership with families. 
- Curriculum: Strong emphasis on Bible, Christian worldview, and arts integration; explicit Great Books/Great Conversation language is not present; liberal-arts canon (grammar, logic, rhetoric) is not foregrounded in public materials. This yields a notable deviation from a textbook classical curriculum. Evidence: Academics, Bible-centered curriculum; Arts emphasis; absence of explicit Great Books/grammar-logic-rhetoric triad in public-facing curriculum. 
- Pedagogy: Time-tested, faith-integrated pedagogy with memory- and performance-oriented elements (chants/singing in lower grades, chapel-based formation, advisement), but lacking explicit evidence of Socratic seminars or rhetoric-centric high-school pedagogy in public materials. This suggests a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL stance rather than fully classical pedagogy. Evidence: Lower-school singing/memory approaches described; upper-school emphasis on chapel-integrated and faith-based formation; absence of explicit Socratic/seminar language in public-facing pedagogy. 
- Faculty: Described as virtuous, caring, and model teachers; living curriculum and mentors; teachers depicted as partners with students in a faith-centered, academically serious environment. This aligns with classical expectations. Evidence: Middle School faculty description; “living curriculum” and teacher-student partnership language. 
- Parents & Community: Strong partnership with families, expectations of parental involvement, and opportunities for parent formation through events and communications. Evidence: Who We Are statements and repeated emphasis on partnership with parents; community engagement described in weekly news and event planning.

### Element 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - Pedagogical practices described include memory-friendly, faith-centered learning (chapels, devotions, Bible study; singing and memory in lower grades) and a strong emphasis on community, advisement, and age-appropriate curricula; high-school pedagogy (Socratic methods, seminars, debates) is not explicitly described in public-facing materials, though the school offers upper-level rhetoric training in some programs (e.g., speech opportunities in broader programs). The public materials emphasize formation and community rather than explicit Socratic seminars or classical rhetoric-based pedagogy. 
  - There is explicit use of singing and memory-work in lower grades and a strong emphasis on “wonder” and experiential learning in some arts programs, but no robust documentation of regular Socratic seminars or formal rhetoric training at the high-school level in public pages. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive keywords present: scholé/restful learning, singing/memory in lower grades, reverence/wonder, chapel-based learning, formal Bible/chapel integration.
  - Negative keywords present: lack of explicit Socratic seminars, lack of formal Great Books or rhetoric-based capstone or oratorical programs in public materials.
  - Estimated frequency: moderate positive signals surrounding contemplative, memory-based, and faith-integrated pedagogy; limited presence of classical-pedagogy markers (Socratic seminars, rhetoric-based assessment).
- Why this rating
  - The school’s pedagogy shows meaningful alignment with contemplative, formation-focused methods (Scholé-like elements and memory work) but does not consistently demonstrate the hallmark classical pedagogy of Socratic seminars, rigorous dialectic, or rhetoric-focused public-speaking programs across the high-school level. This yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rating for Pedagogy. See Spiritual Formation and Middle/Elementary academic pages for evidence of memory-based and faith-integrated pedagogy; lack of explicit Socratic/seminar language in public content supports the nuanced ranking.

What Would Move the Needle (note: applied only if SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL; not required for CLASSICAL)

- For Element 4 (Pedagogy): Adopt a more explicit classical-pedagogy framework, incorporating Socratic seminars, dialectic activities, and formal rhetoric instruction in upper grades; integrate regular, planned opportunities for students to deliver orations, debates, and public speaking with guided feedback. 
- For Element 3 (Curriculum): Introduce a formal liberal-arts sequence (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and clearly map a "Great Books/Great Conversation" track within the upper grades; include Latin or Greek options and a canonical Western Civ sequence to align with classical canon. 
- For Element 4 (Pedagogy) and Element 3 (Curriculum): Provide explicit alignment language on the website that ties daily practice to classical-pedagogy markers (scholé days, memory work, recitation, memorized passages, etc.). 
- For Communications: Add a public-facing summary of the Great Books/Gra mat rhetoric approach to ensure prospective families understand the classical goals and how the school intends to implement them.
