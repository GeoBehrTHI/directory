## Executive Summary

Dogwood Classical Academy NC 28115 presents a clearly classical orientation with a strong emphasis on virtue, truth-seeking, and a Great Books–driven curriculum framed by Socratic dialogue. Public materials emphasize formation of mind and character, a pledge that foregrounds the transcendental triad of truth, goodness, and beauty, and a staged approach to learning that reflects the Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) in practice. However, explicit theological grounding, liturgical or communal religious practices, and detailed descriptions of faculty as the living curriculum, or a stated, proactive Parents & Community program, are not readily evident in the available public materials. Taken together, the school demonstrates many core classical elements but does not fully articulate all six categories in the fullest classical sense. Overall assessment: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Key observations driving this assessment include:

- Explicit commitment to a classical, Great Books–driven curriculum and to Socratic dialogue as a pedagogical approach (evidence in mission/vision content and curriculum descriptions) , .
- A School Pledge that grounds education in truth, goodness, and beauty, illustrating transcendental moral realism that aligns with classical aims (evidence in mission/vision content) .
- Clear formative aims (formation of mind and character, virtuous living, civic responsibility) rather than vocational preparation, aligning with the classical purpose emphasis, but with limited explicit theological or liturgical language in the public materials (Element 1; Element 2) .
- Pedagogy evidences include K–12 structure with memory/recitation in early grades and Socratic/communicative emphasis in higher grades, consistent with the Trivium framework (Element 4) .
- Gaps identified around explicit theology, liturgical/community practices, detailed “living curriculum” faculty portrayal, and formal parental/community partnership language in the public materials (Categories 1, 5, 6) .

Executive Summary — Element-by-Element Snapshot

### Element 1: Religious and Metaphysical Foundations — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - Public materials foreground truth, goodness, and beauty as guiding ideals through the School Pledge: “I will learn what is true, I will do what is good, and I will love what is beautiful.” This articulates transcendental moral realism.
    - The overall mission/vision emphasizes virtuous living and Western heritage through a classical curriculum and Socratic dialogue, signaling a metaphysical orientation toward virtue and timeless truths.
    - The Great Books and Socratic dialogue components align with a real-knowledge, real-virtue framework and the pursuit of enduring truths.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive: truth, true, goodness, beautiful/beauty, virtue, virtuous, character, Socratic dialogue, great ideas, Great Books, formation of mind and character.
    - Negative: explicit theology, liturgy, liturgical practices; explicit religious/theological grounding is not evident in the public materials (no clear wording about theology or liturgical worship within the materials reviewed).

- Why this rating
    - The presence of transcendental moral realism (truth, goodness, beauty) and virtue-centered formation aligns with Classical aims in spirit, though the materials do not show explicit theological grounding or liturgical/community practices. Given these gaps, Element 1 sits in SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL.

Citations: Pledge language and moral triad are visible in the school materials; explicit theology/ liturgical language is not.

### Element 2: School Purpose and Goal — CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - Explicit focus on forming mind and character through virtuous living and civic responsibility, with a curriculum described as content-rich and classical, rather than training for a job. This indicates formation over information-oriented aims.
    - The pledge and the mission emphasize truth, goodness, and beauty as real ideals guiding education, supporting a philosophical stance that knowledge is oriented toward human flourishing and virtue.
    - The pedagogy emphasizes formation and habits (Socratic dialogue, inquiry, and Great Books) rather than vocational outcomes alone.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive: wisdom, virtue, character, integrity, love of learning, Socratic dialogue, great conversation, ethical reasoning, mentorship, formation, practical notion of human flourishing, sacred/tradition language (indirectly via Western heritage and classical ideals).
    - Negative: none explicit about secular/vocational aims; the public materials do not foreground job- or skill-based outcomes.

- Why this rating
    - The materials clearly articulate formation, virtue, and the existence of truth/goodness/beauty as real ideals guiding education. Vocational aims are not foregrounded; the emphasis is on formation and virtue over labor-market preparation. This aligns with CLASSICAL in Element 2.

Citations: Mission/vision language and pledge supporting formation and transcendental aims.

### Element 3: School Curriculum — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - The curriculum is described as classical and Great Books–rich, with a deliberate emphasis on a three-stage learning process (grammar-like foundation, then reasoning, then rhetoric/communication). The materials describe K–2 memorization and language-based learning, 6–8 reasoning with connections, and 9–12 focus on advanced topics and effective communication. This reflects the Trivium-style progression.
    - The Vision explicitly references a Great Books curriculum and Socratic dialogue, signaling content choices aligned with classical liberal arts aims.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive: Great Books, classical curriculum, Socratic dialogue, great books, liberal arts, rhetoric (implied by emphasis on communicating effectively), memory/recitation (grammar-stage behavior), formation of habits in language, history, and arts/sciences (integrated approach).
    - Negative: explicit naming of the three liberal arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric) is not always explicit in every public snippet, though the pedagogy implies Trivium-like structure; no evident negative curricular descriptors.

- Why this rating
    - The school clearly embraces classical curricular aims (Great Books, content-rich classical curriculum, Socratic inquiry). However, explicit, named references to the liberal arts of grammar, logic, and rhetoric as the formal curricular content are not always spelled out in a single, explicit list; the staged approach covers similar ground. Given that, this category is BEST described as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than fully CLASSICAL.

Citations: Great Books, Socratic dialogue, and three-stage progression visible in mission/vision content.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - Public materials emphasize Socratic dialogue and inquiry as core pedagogical methods, especially in the upper levels. This aligns with classical pedagogy (dialectic, inquiry, seminar-style learning).
    - The lower grades are described as memory- and story-based with guided discussion, which corresponds to the grammar stage and deliberate memory work; upper grades emphasize discussion and rhetorical development.
    - There is explicit reference to students being trained in rhetoric and having opportunities to give speeches in the upper grades (rhetorical training evident in the description of the third stage).

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive: Socratic dialogue, seminars, debates (explicit), rhetoric, quiet/restful scholarly inquiry (implied through scholé-inspired thinking in broader literature), great books (content focus), wonder (as a learning aim in classical pedagogy).
    - Negative: no explicit mention of overemphasis on project-based or overly modern, non-classical pedagogies; the public materials emphasize classical methods rather than contemporary alternatives.

- Why this rating
    - The school’s pedagogy aligns squarely with classical methods (Socratic dialogue, rhetoric, and a staged approach to learning). The presence of lower-grade memory/chanting analogies is suggested but not explicitly stated as chant/singing; nonetheless, the overall pedagogy is clearly classical, supporting a CLASSICAL rating for Element 4.

Citations: Socratic dialogue and rhetoric emphasis in mission/vision and curriculum descriptions.

### Element 5: School Faculty — NOT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - Public materials do not provide explicit statements about faculty as living curriculum, faculty portraits, or a verifiable “profile of what a teacher should be” at the school. The available public content centers on curriculum, pledge, and governance statements rather than detailed faculty characterizations or a portrait of teacher role models within the school culture.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive keywords (from Six Essentials): virtuous teachers, mentors, role models, living curriculum, teacher profiles. The Dogwood materials do not clearly present these terms or concepts in the public materials.
    - Negative keywords: none explicit in the Dogwood materials that would actively negate this category (i.e., no explicit negative statements about teachers); rather, there is a lack of explicit statements.

- Why this rating
    - The absence of explicit content about faculty as a living curriculum or portraits of teachers prevents a CLASSICAL rating in Element 5. Given the rubric, this category is NOT CLASSICAL for this school based on available public content.

Citations: Lack of explicit “living curriculum” faculty statements in the public materials; primary evidence centers on curriculum and pledge.

### Element 6: Parents & Community — NOT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
    - The public materials reviewed do not show a robust, explicit program of parent partnership as co-teachers, formal education of parents via book studies or gatherings, or open invitation for parents to visit “virtually any time.” The available excerpts focus on student formation, curriculum, and promise language rather than a structured family/community engagement program.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
    - Positive indicators (from the Six Essentials): “partners with parents,” “parents as co-teachers,” “educate parents via website/book studies/gatherings,” “parents welcome for visits.”
    - Negative indicators: absence of such phrases in public materials; lack of explicit parent-co-teacher language or programs.

- Why this rating
    - Because the school’s public materials do not demonstrate explicit parent/community engagement structures or invitations, Element 6 is NOT CLASSICAL.

Citations: The materials reviewed emphasize student-centered classical aims rather than explicit Parent & Community programs.

What Would Move the Needle (for SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL schools)

- Element 1 (Religious and Metaphysical Foundations)
    - Add explicit theological framing or a clearly stated theological tradition (e.g., Hillsdale/Barney Charter alignment with a substantive theological grounding) and reference liturgical or communal practices that integrate worship or ritual as a regular part of school life.

- Element 3 (Curriculum)
    - Make explicit the Trivium components by naming grammar, logic, and rhetoric as formal curricular strands, with targeted outcomes and sample units for each stage. Provide clarity on Great Books selections and their role in forming the Great Conversation as a concrete, published curriculum map.

- Element 5 (Faculty)
    - Publish a living-curriculum portrait of faculty and a formal statement about teacher formation, mentorship roles, and professional norms; include a description of how faculty embody virtue, skill, and knowledge; provide examples of faculty professional development and collegial practices.

- Element 6 (Parents & Community)
    - Establish and advertise structured parent-partner programs (co-teaching opportunities, book studies, parent evenings, and regular in-person/virtual visits). Create explicit channels for parent education about classical education and how parents participate as co-teachers.

- Cross-cutting
    - Increase explicit references to theology or liturgical practices if appropriate to the school’s mission and charter, to strengthen Element 1 and align with more robust classical norms.
    - Provide a public, consistent articulation of the school’s philosophy of education (Scholé/harmonia of rest and work) with examples of how rest, contemplation, and wonder are woven into daily practice (as supported by the broader Scholé literature in the shared resources).
