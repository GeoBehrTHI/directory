## Executive Summary

Trinitas Classical Academy (TN 37064) presents as a clearly Christian, classical-leaning school with a publicly stated focus on character formation, classical identity, and a values-based community. Public-facing materials describe Trinitas as a “Christian and Classical” school with a focus on small classes, personalized instruction, and a Christian worldview. While the school explicitly brands itself as classical, the accessible content emphasizes religious grounding and general classical framing without consistently naming core classical elements such as the liberal arts sequence (grammar, logic, rhetoric), Great Books/Great Conversation, or explicit Socratic pedagogy across the full grades. Based on the Six Essential Elements rubric, the school lands in the “Somewhat Classical” category overall, with strongest alignment in Religious Foundations and Community/Formation orientation, and more limited explicit alignment in the explicit curriculum and pedagogy markers that define a fully classical program.

Key supporting observations from the school’s public materials include:
- A blending of Christian worldview with classical imagery, including phrases like “Christian Worldview & Classical Education” and “kingdom of God” language signaling theological grounding (Element 1). 
- Explicit branding as “Classical Christian Education” and emphasis on formation, virtue, and a values-based community (Element 2). 
- Curriculum framing as classical in identity, but without explicit public references to Great Books, grammar/logic/rhetoric sequence, or a universal Great Conversation across grades (Element 3). 
- Pedagogical notes in the materials emphasize traditional classroom values (small classes, personalized instruction) but not explicit Socratic seminars or scholé-based restfulness across the full program (Element 4). 
- Faculty messaging is traditional in tone (highly qualified teachers, caring culture) but lacks explicit “living curriculum” language or formal faculty portraits in the accessible materials (Element 5). 
- Parental/Community engagement is present (tours, openness, parent involvement in events and communications), consistent with classical-school expectations of parental partnership, though explicit “co-teaching” language is not consistently foregrounded in the public materials (Element 6).

## What Would Move the Needle

- Curriculum clarity: Publicly enumerate and weave into the website the explicit classical-curriculum elements (grammar, logic, rhetoric; history through Western civilization; Latin/Greek where applicable; Great Books and the Great Conversation). This would elevate Element 3 to CLASSICAL or at least STRONGER SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.
- Pedagogy articulation: Introduce and describe explicit classical teaching methods (Socratic seminars, memorization drills, narration/recitation, scholé/restful-learning days, etc.) with concrete examples across grades. This would shift Element 4 toward CLASSICAL.
- Liturgical/community practices: If appropriate to the school’s ethos, articulate liturgical or communal practices (prayer, liturgy, communal music, or other traditions) as part of the Religious Foundations and Pedagogy categories, linking to the lexicon’s liturgy/chant/worship indicators. This would strengthen Element 1 and Element 4.
- Living curriculum language: Incorporate language about a “living curriculum” modeled by faculty, with portraits/character expectations for teachers and a visible plan for teacher development in classical pedagogy. This would bolster Element 5 toward CLASSICAL.
- Parental formation programming: Expand explicit parent education (reading groups, book studies, family liturgy alignment) and clearly present these opportunities on the site, moving Element 6 closer to CLASSICAL.
- Great Books and Great Conversation emphasis: Provide explicit references to Great Books, classical authors, and the Great Conversation in the school’s stated aims, mission, and course descriptions. This would move several categories—especially Curriculum and Purpose—toward CLASSICAL.
