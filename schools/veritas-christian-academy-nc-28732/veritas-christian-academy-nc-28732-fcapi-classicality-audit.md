## Executive Summary

Veritas Christian Academy (Fletcher, NC) presents itself publicly as a Christian, classical school with a PreK–12 program and a curriculum anchored in the Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and a Christ-centered, Biblically-informed framework. The website emphasizes intelligent faith, character formation, and a strong faculty-student relationship grounded in Christian formation. While the school clearly adopts core classical aims (classical curriculum, Trivium, and a devotional/ liturgical ethos evidenced by events like Lessons & Carols), it does not overtly publicize several hallmark practices frequently associated with a fully classical public stance (e.g., explicit Great Books program, explicit Socratic seminars, a published living curriculum portrait, or robust, explicit parental-partnership programs on the site). Taken together, the public materials align most strongly with Classical or strongly Classical elements in categories tied to religious/metaphysical foundations and curricular language, but evidence for full implementation across all six classical elements is incomplete on the site. This yields a general assessment of SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall, with particular strength in Religious and Metaphysical Foundations and Curriculum, and opportunities for growth in Parent/Community engagement and explicit Socratic pedagogy.

Key drivers behind this assessment:
- The site foregrounds Intelligent Faith, a Christ-centered approach, and a Trivium-based Classical curriculum, indicating a robust religious/metaphysical foundation and a curriculum built on classical methods. Evidence includes explicit statements like “Intelligent Faith: Equipping minds and engaging hearts for God's Kingdom” and “built on the classical Trivium and historic Biblical Christianity.” 
- The mission and tone emphasize formation and holistic nurture (the “whole child”) within a classical framework, which aligns with classical aims in School Purpose & Goal, albeit with less explicit articulation of some classical classics-centered practices (Great Books, Great Conversation) on the public-facing pages. 
- Pedagogy, liturgical rhythm, and community display (e.g., public events like Lessons & Carols) show an integration of liturgical practices into schooling, which supports Element 1’s liturgical dimension but leaves Socratic/seminar-based pedagogy less explicit in public content. 
- Keyword analysis against the Six Essential Elements lexicon (positive/negative indicators) confirms substantial alignment in several categories, but with gaps in publicly stated, explicit Great Books/Great Conversation language and formal parental partnership language on the site. See the classical lexicon for reference to the relevant keywords. 

Note: The analysis that follows systematically applies a keyword-based rubric (Positive/Negative keyword indicators from the Six Essential Elements) to each of the six classical elements, then assigns a category rating, and finally offers needle-moving steps if warranted.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive keywords present: grammar/logic/rehtoric (Trivium) on the curriculum side implies a pedagogy aligned with those arts; liturgical learning and communal practices are present in public materials (via events such as Lessons & Carols), signaling an embodied pedagogy. The absence of explicit statements to these modern pedagogies supports a more classical reading. 
- Why this rating
  - The school’s stated identity as “Christian Classical” with Trivium-based framing supports a pedagogical orientation consistent with classical learning, but the public-facing content does not clearly enumerate the full suite of classical pedagogical practices (Socratic seminars, scholé days, etc.). Hence, SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL is warranted.

Citations: Lexicon anchors for pedagogical techniques (Socratic dialogue, scholé, liturgical learning); Veritas site for Trivium-based pedagogy cues.

## What Would Move the Needle

- Element 1 (Religious and Metaphysical Foundations): Publicly articulate a succinct, explicit statement of liturgical rhythm and theological grounding across disciplines (e.g., “Mistress Science” framing; explicit reference to liturgical practices across the day). This would strengthen the liturgical/commun al foundations and deepen transcendental realism in public materials.
- Element 2 (School Purpose and Goal): Publish a public telos/paideia statement that ties formation, wisdom, and the true/good/beautiful to observable outcomes (student character formation, virtue development), with explicit mention of Great Conversation and Great Books integration.
- Element 3 (Curriculum): Publish a formal curriculum map that enumerates the Seven Liberal Arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric; arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music), Great Books/go-to canonical texts by grade levels, and the explicit integration of Great Books into history and literature, including primary sources. This would move Element 3 toward CLASSICAL.
- Element 4 (Pedagogy): Publicly commit to specific classical pedagogies—Socratic discussion in high school, disciplined memory work, memorization/recitation routines, and scholé days—and provide examples of daily/weekly lesson forms used in K–12.
- Element 5 (Faculty): Publish a “Portrait of a Classical Teacher” and a “Living Curriculum” narrative to demonstrate how teachers embody virtue and act as mentors, with faculty formation and collaboration showcased publicly.
- Element 6 (Parents & Community): Establish and publicize a formal parent-partnership program (co-teaching, parent education, school visits, book clubs) and open community events to strengthen parent engagement.

Citations for context and framework: The Lexicon’s indicators for all six elements (Religious, Purposes, Curriculum, Pedagogy, Faculty, Parents/Community) provide a structured template for what to publish publicly to push toward CLASSICAL in each category.
