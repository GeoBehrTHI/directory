## Executive Summary

Mount Tabor School of the Liberal Arts (Kansas City, MO) presents a robust classical education anchored in Orthodox Christian faith. Public materials consistently frame the school as a liberal-arts, classical program with explicit religious foundations, a clear sense of formation over mere information, and a pedagogy that blends contemplative elements with Socratic inquiry. The curriculum foregrounds the liberal arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), engagement with classical texts, and a liturgical, communally oriented life. Across categories, the school demonstrates strong Classical indicators, with Element 4 (Pedagogy) representing the area of relative nuance where the school shows both classical (Socratic discourse, rhetoric) and non-classical elements (some emphasis on structured schedules and liturgical life) that suggest it is best described as CLASSICAL with a notable Somewhat Classical element in pedagogy.

Key indicators across the six classical categories:

- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations: Strong. Orthodox Christian framing, liturgical worship, and directed spiritual formation are explicit. Evidence includes parish affiliation, weekly liturgy, and spiritual readings tied to saints and Church Fathers.  
- Purpose and Goal: Strong. Emphasis on formation, virtue, wisdom, and lifelong character shaped within a Christian, classical framework; explicit statements about forming leaders and keeping the faith. 
- Curriculum: Strong. Liberal arts core (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and Greek immersion; explicit reference to classical texts, Great Books/Conversations flavor via “Encounter the Classics” language and “great conversation” framing in the materials.  
- Pedagogy: Mixed but convergent with classical norms. Socratic discussion in upper grades and rhetoric training referenced; lower grades feature a communal, liturgical life that aligns with scholé-like elements rather than pure restfulness in every instance. Overall, pedagogy leans CLASSICAL with some non-traditional scheduling and liturgical components. 
- Faculty: Strong. Leadership and staff pages show a team of committed, mission-aligned teachers and administrators; the staff is positioned as the living curriculum and core of the school, with a parish-centered identity. 
- Parents & Community: Strong. Emphasizes partnership with parents, liturgical participation, and communal life anchored in parish life; parent involvement is framed as an ongoing, welcomed element of school life. 

Overall aggregate: CLASSICAL. The school demonstrates the hallmarks of classical education—rooted in truth, goodness, beauty, and formation—within an Orthodox Christian framework, with explicit liberal-arts coursework, Great Books-oriented emphasis, and Socratic pedagogy in the upper grades.

- The school speaks of “the liberal arts” as a framework—Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and additional classical components (Greek immersion; study of saints and Church Fathers; primary sources).

- Negative keywords: social studies terminology replacing history; project-based or experiential-dominant learning (not present as primary in the public materials). The materials emphasize classical texts and integrated study rather than modern purely project-based pedagogy.

Why this rating
- The explicit presence of the seven liberal-arts frame (grammar, logic, rhetoric; Greek immersion), engagement with classics, and integrated humanities strongly aligns with CLASSICAL curriculum expectations.

Citations: Curriculum descriptions (Academics, About, and firecrawl materials) show Liberal Arts emphasis, grammar/logic/rhetoric, and ancient Greek immersion.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Socratic discussion is explicitly referenced for upper grades (Socratic discussion, seminars, or debates; use of logic). This is a core Classical pedagogy element for higher grades. 
- The school maintains a strong ritual/communal component (weekly liturgy and choir) that shapes memory, ethics, and formation, which aligns with scholé-like and contemplative elements but is not exclusively “restful learning” in the classical sense. 
- The curriculum emphasizes rhetoric and public speaking or rhetorical practice in upper grades; the site describes a classical curriculum oriented toward language arts and debate. 
- There is strong evidence of two distinct modalities: a liturgical, communal rhythm (weekly worship, sacred arts) and high-school Socratic/seminar-style inquiry. The presence of liturgical life (which is not a standard “pedagogy method” in all classical models) reduces the purity of a purely Socratic model, placing it in a blended, somewhat classical category. 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: Socratic discussion, seminars, rhetoric, oratory, debate, memory work, recitation (labeled in the Classical Lexicon as appropriate upper-level pedagogy), chanting/singing as a memory aid,教学 approaches that emphasize classical dialogue and memory.
- Negative keywords present: experiential learning-focused, project-based learning dominant (not described as the sole or primary pedagogy here); restful scholé days are not emphasized across the public materials (though some contemplation exists through liturgical life).

Why this rating
- Pedagogy shows strong classical components (Socratic discourse and rhetoric) but is blended with liturgical and communal practices that are not purely pedagogical methods. This hybrid yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rating in pedagogy.

Citations: Socratic/dialectic references (upper grades) and the presence of liturgical/choir activities (which shape pedagogy through culture and memory).

## What Would Move the Needle

- If the Pedagogy category remains the only somewhat-classical area (Element 4), the following needle-moving steps could push Pedagogy toward a fully Classical profile:
  - Increase explicit articulation of classical pedagogy across all grades (e.g., explicit mention of memory work, recitation, narration, and Socratic seminars in lower grades as well as upper grades).
  - Expand explicit Great Books/Great Conversation integration beyond wording in overview to concrete reading lists and seminar structures for K–12 (consistent naming of Great Books and Great Conversation on the site and in curricula).
  - Clarify a more explicit, contemplative Scholé orientation in daily routines (e.g., dedicated “Scholé days” with explicit restfulness and reflective practices) and connect these to memory and virtue formation.
  - Provide more detailed, public-facing rationale for why liturgical life and sacred music are integrated pedagogy rather than separate Christian life components, to show a fully integrated classical approach rather than a supplement.
  - Increase public-facing evidence of formal classical pedagogy in lower grades (e.g., lower-grade Socratic-style inquiry and simple rhetoric practice) to demonstrate continuity of classical methods from K–12.

Citations: Pedagogy elements evidenced (Socratic discussion, private rhetoric/upper-grade focus) are described in the site materials.
