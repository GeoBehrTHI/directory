## Executive Summary

Saint Thomas' Episcopal School (STE) presents a robust Christian classical model in Houston, TX 77096, grounded in a Christ-centered worldview and a long-standing emphasis on liberal arts, memory work, and a liturgical culture. Public-facing materials consistently foreground a classical frame (e.g., “classical education,” “liberal arts,” “worship in chapel,” “daily chapel,” and a mission to form honorable men and women through a classical education grounded in a Christ-centered worldview). Evidence from the STE site highlights strong religious underpinnings, formation goals, a clearly articulated Catholic/Anglican-tinged Christian ethos, and a community-oriented approach that explicitly partners with families. Overall, the public materials align with classical aims in many respects, though some pedagogical specifics (e.g., explicit Socratic seminars or a full Great Books emphasis) are less explicit on the site and require inference from related STE materials.

- Category-by-category consensus: CLASSICAL in Religious & Metaphysical Foundations; CLASSICAL in School Purpose & Goal; SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL in Curriculum; SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL in Pedagogy; CLASSICAL in Faculty; CLASSICAL in Parents & Community.
- Aggregate assessment: CLASSICAL.

Key evidence includes explicit statements about truth, goodness, beauty; a Christ-centered worldview; liturgical worship and a Book of Common Prayer; a mission of forming honorable men and women through classical education; a liberal-arts orientation; and ongoing faculty-led mentorship and family partnerships. See sources cited in each category below for concrete quotes and pages that ground these judgments.

Why this rating

This maps cleanly to the Classical category per the rubric’s indicators.

Citations reflect explicit phrases and practices cited above.

### Element 3: Curriculum — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence

- Grades 1–5 materials emphasize a foundation in literacy, arithmetic, grammar, and also a Christian worldview through systematic worship and doctrine. This demonstrates a liberal-arts-leaning foundation in the lower grades and a pathway toward a liberal-arts curriculum in middle/upper grades.
- The lower/upper progression mentions an accelerated liberal arts curriculum in Middle and Upper schools, indicating a movement toward more classical disciplines over time.
- Materials emphasize a Christian worldview integrated with academic study and arts, suggesting an integrated, holistic approach rather than a strictly modern, social-studies-leaning curriculum.
- There are explicit references to a classical education framework (mission statements and school identity). The site also highlights arts (including Scottish Arts) as part of the curriculum identity, which aligns with broader classical education values, though not every Great-Books or Seven Liberal Arts component is spelled out explicitly on the site.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: liberal arts, classical education, history/philosophy/arts/science as a unified core, and a binding emphasis on formation through study of Christian doctrine. The explicit articulation of a “liberal arts curriculum” and a pathway toward upper-level classical study aligns with Element 3’s Positive Keywords in the Six Essentials framework.
- Negative keywords present: not overtly present on the public pages; however, explicit mentions of Great Books, the Great Conversation, or a rigorous Seven Liberal Arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric; quadrivium) are not consistently spelled out on the STE site. The presence of “accelerated liberal arts curriculum” but without explicit seven liberal arts articulation is a mild gap.

Why this rating

- The curriculum shows strong classical alignment (liberal arts orientation, integrated study, and formation through study), but it does not consistently and explicitly enumerate all canonical classical elements (e.g., seven liberal arts, Great Books, explicit Great Conversation) in the public materials. Thus, categorized as Somewhat Classical.

Citations: lower/upper curriculum description and “accelerated liberal arts curriculum” references.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence

- The site emphasizes a liturgical and communal dimension (chapel, Bible study) and strong arts involvement; this is consistent with a contemplative/restful or ritually integrated pedagogical approach characteristic of scholé-inspired teaching in some classical programs. The public materials describe a reverent, community-centered approach to education.
- There are explicit references to a traditional, Christ-centered classical curriculum that includes a memory/recitation component in the broader classical framework (as seen in related classical education discussions within STE materials, including “learning humility through the classics” and Great Conversation themes). In STE’s public materials, specific, explicit mention of Socratic seminars or high-school-level rhetoric seminars is not consistently visible, though the school emphasizes classical training and a disciplined, rigorous approach. The Scotland Arts program and related activities emphasize structured performance-based and memory-based components (e.g., music, pipe band, choruses).
- The Center for Academic Excellence indicates differentiated instruction, study strategies, and supports for students with varied needs, which is aligned with a holistic pedagogy that may include contemplative or restful elements alongside rigorous work.
- The public-facing materials lack consistent, explicit statements of traditional classical pedagogy (Socratic seminars, recitation, or living books as a formal, school-wide practice in every grade). When such elements are present in the broader STE materials (e.g., classical formation through the classics, Great Books discourse in admissions materials), they are not always stated in the core classroom pedagogy pages.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: contemplative learning, scholé-like rhythms (explicitly mentioned in the Six Essentials framework as a positive), memorization/recitation, chanting, liturgical rhythm, chorus, and rhetoric training appear in the canonical Six Essentials lists. STE’s materials show contemplative worship and memory-centered activities (e.g., annual Scottish Arts with performance memory elements) though explicit “Socratic seminars” or “living books” language is not consistently foregrounded.
- Negative keywords present: some public materials do not foreground project-based or exclusively experiential learning as the sole method; the emphasis remains on classical formation through disciplined study and liturgical/community practices. This aligns with a mixed pedagogy rather than a strictly modern, project-based explicit pedagogy.

Why this rating

- STE demonstrates strong classical underpinnings (memory, liturgical rhythm, disciplined study, and an arts-centric, community-oriented pedagogy) but with less explicit, explicit articulation of canonical techniques (Socratic seminars, structured living books, or the seven liberal arts pedagogy across all grades) in the public-facing materials. Thus, categorized as Somewhat Classical.

Citations: notes on liturgical/communal practices, and the absence/presence of explicit Socratic-style pedagogy.

Overall Assessment

- Aggregate classification: CLASSICAL.
- Rationale: Across six categories, STE demonstrates strong alignment with classical education expectations in Religious Foundations, School Purpose, Faculty, and Parents & Community. The Curriculum and Pedagogy categories show substantial classical elements but are not always explicit in canonical Great Books, Seven Liberal Arts, or Socratic seminar language on the public site. Those elements are present in the broader STE materials and mission rhetoric, but the public-facing pages emphasize formation, liberal arts, and liturgical/communal life more than a full canonical catalog of classical pedagogy. As such, the school sits firmly in CLASSICAL, with some room to strengthen explicit articulation of certain canonical classical curriculum and pedagogy components.

What Would Move the Needle (for SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL categories)

- Pedagogy: Increase explicit articulation and evidence of Socratic seminars and living-books-based instruction in high school, and clearly document a plan for Socratic inquiry in the upper grades on the public site. Provide a public, grade-by-grade map showing how grammar-logic-rhetoric (the Trivium) and the Quadrivium are integrated across grades, including sample Great Books selections or canonical texts to illustrate the Great Conversation.
- Curriculum: Make explicit in public materials the Great Books framework, canonical texts (great books), and a clear Seven Liberal Arts pathway (grammar, logic, rhetoric; arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) across the grades, along with a cross-disciplinary integration narrative showing how history, literature, philosophy, and sciences interact in the STE curriculum.
- Public-facing evidence of Great Books: Include a dedicated page or section that lists core classical texts, authors, and accompanying discussions or projects to demonstrate a structured Great Conversation approach.
