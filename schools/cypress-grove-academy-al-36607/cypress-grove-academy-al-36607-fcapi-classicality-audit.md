## Executive Summary

Cypress Grove Academy (CGA) presents a clearly Christian, classical educational identity framed around a Christ-centered mission, shared virtue, and a cooperative model with families. Public materials emphasize classical aims (e.g., pursuit of truth, beauty, and goodness; formation of character and virtue; integration of faith with learning) and a governance that centers on theology and Scripture. The school also foregrounds a collaborative, parental-partnering approach, a statement of faith, and a family-inclusive culture, including a hybrid class structure and a house-oriented community.

Overall, the school aligns strongly with classical aims in religious foundations, purpose, and faculty/parent/community orientations. The curriculum and pedagogy show clear classical scaffolding (Trivium orientation, memorable recitation and formation-through-models themes) but are less explicit about some hallmark practices (notably explicit, public-facing Great Books or explicit Socratic/seminar pedagogy in all grades). Taken as a whole, Cypress Grove Academy is CLASSICAL, with room to further illuminate certain pedagogical/textual elements to move toward full alignment in every dimension.

- Religious & Metaphysical Foundations: CLASSICAL
- Purpose and Goal: CLASSICAL
- Curriculum: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Pedagogy: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Faculty: CLASSICAL
- Parents & Community: CLASSICAL

What would move the needle for categories rated SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL is to deepen explicit articulation and public-facing evidence of (a) Great Books/Great Conversation elements within the curriculum and (b) explicitly stated Socratic/dialogic pedagogy or scholé-based instructional practices in the upper grades. Concrete steps to strengthen alignment are described in the “What Would Move the Needle” section below.

Evidence-based observations and keyword analyses draw on the school’s public materials and the classical education rubric in the knowledge base, including The Six Essential Elements of a Classical School (1) and related lexical guides.

Some elements of Great Books/Great Conversation are present in the Six Essential Elements corpus and related lexicon, but explicit CGA public-facing statements confirming Great Books participation are not consistently shown in the crawl results.   

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: grammar, logic, rhetoric (Trivium); Seven Liberal Arts; history across the curriculum (integrated humanities emphasis); wonder; great conversation (referenced in the canonical elements as a positive in classical education). Evident in explicit Trivium framing and liberal-arts orientation.  
- Negative keywords present: some materials emphasize project-based or experiential learning in the broader lexicon, and the school’s explicit site content does not consistently foreground Great Books or direct Great-Conversation language; this suggests partial alignment rather than full public-facing Great-Books emphasis. This creates a partial misalignment with the strongest classical-curriculum signals in the Six Elements corpus. 

Why this rating
- The presence of Trivium framing and liberal-arts orientation is strong evidence for CLASSICAL curriculum, but the public site content does not consistently foreground Great Books/Great Conversation or the explicit, comprehensive Great-Books canon, which keeps this category at SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL. If the school publishes explicit Great Books materials on the site or in syllabi, it could move to CLASSICAL; as of now, the evidence supports SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL with room for stronger public articulation of Great Books and related Great Conversation commitments. 

What would move the needle (for Category 3)

### Category 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Pedagogy signals in public materials emphasize a blend of collaborative, teacher-led instruction with a collaborative/hybrid model (students attend classes three days a week with at-home assignments graded by classroom teachers). The ritual aspects of classical pedagogy (e.g., restfulness, contemplative time) are not consistently highlighted, though there are elements of formation and community that align with classical aims. 
- The school speaks to partnering with parents and a culture of virtue and formation, which can support a pedagogy oriented toward formation over mere information. The presence of a “gospel-centered, classical” approach suggests a pedagogy oriented toward the unity of truth, virtue, and beauty rather than purely vocational outcomes. 
- The formal pedagogy signals (Socratic seminars, memory work, recitation, and rest in pedagogy) are part of the Six Essential Elements framework; however, the public materials for CGA do not consistently depict explicit Socratic/dialectic seminars or scholé-style instruction across grades. The Great Books/Great Conversation orientation is not consistently explicit on the site. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: Socratic dialogue, dialectic, great conversation, guided discussion, memory work, recitation, scholé, restfulness, contemplation, rhetorical training, public speaking and oratory, disciplined dialogue. The school’s materials emphasize classical pedagogy foundations in mission and faculty pages, and the Six Elements provide a vocabulary for this pedagogy. 
- Negative keywords present: the site does not consistently foreground project-based or experiential-only pedagogy, and the explicit public-facing articulation of Socratic seminars or Scholé is not ubiquitous. Some results suggest a hybrid model, not exclusively Socratic. This partially aligns with classical pedagogy signals but is not uniformly demonstrated on the site. 

Why this rating
- The program’s design supports formation and classical aims (Trivium-centered method, memory work, recitation, and a collaborative, faith-based context). However, explicit, frequent public-facing evidence of Socratic seminars, Scholé-based instruction, or a consistent upper-school emphasis on dialectic debate across grades is not uniformly present in the crawl content. This yields SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL in Pedagogy.

What would move the needle
- Publish explicit, scalable examples of Socratic seminars or Paideia-style seminars in upper grades, with sample seminar prompts and rubrics, on the public site.
- Publicly foreground Scholé (restful learning) as a regular instructional design principle with examples of contemplative time, memory work and chant/singing as part of daily routines in lower grades.
- Provide representative daily/weekly schedules that illustrate a balance between contemplative, memory-based activities and higher-order discussion in middle/high school.

Also explicitly confirm the continuous integration of history across the curriculum as part of a liberal-arts framework, avoiding a “social studies” framing in core marketing content.

- Category 1 (Religious/Metaphysical Foundations): Maintain the robust theological framing and consider adding a brief public-facing section that highlights liturgical or communal practices (e.g., regular chapel/worship opportunities, liturgical rhythms) to strengthen the liturgical aspect of the religious foundation, if applicable to the school’s actual practice.
