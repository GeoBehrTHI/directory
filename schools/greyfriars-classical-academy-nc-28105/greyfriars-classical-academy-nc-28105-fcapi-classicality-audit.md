## Executive Summary

Greyfriars Classical Academy (GCA) presents a clearly Christian, classical education model blended with a university-style schedule and a Trivium-oriented curriculum. Public materials describe a Christian, covenantal framework anchored in theology (including Westminster Confession of Faith) and a curriculum that emphasizes grammar, logic, and rhetoric, along with history, literature, languages, and theology. The school emphasizes formation of character and wisdom in service of a biblical worldview, with explicit statements about forming “intelligent thinkers” who think God’s thoughts after Him and about shaping future generations for the Kingdom. The faculty are described as qualified, mission-driven teachers who constitute the living curriculum, and there is explicit emphasis on partnering with Christian families. The overall public messaging aligns with a Classical/Christian model and demonstrates many core elements of a classical education, though some elements central to a full Great Books-centric formation are less explicit in the site materials.

- Strong Classical/Christian orientation (theology integrated with disciplines; covenantal framing; Westminster Confession of Faith; biblical worldview). Evidence: explicit theology integration and public articulation of the Statement of Faith; biblical worldview pervading subjects.  
- Curriculum emphasizes grammar, logic, rhetoric and a university-model schedule (on-campus and home study days) with History, Literature, Math, Science, Greek/Spanish, Theology, and Aesthetics. Evidence: curriculum listing and university-model schedule.  
- Faculty described as mission-driven, “living curriculum,” with biographies and explicit emphasis on Christian/classical formation. Evidence: Our Faculty page and bios. 
- Parent/community emphasis appears as partnership with families and open pathways for parent involvement, including a parent handbook and emphasis on collaboration with Christian families. Evidence: Parent-focused handbook language and mission statements. 

Abstract assessment: CLASSICAL overall, with some room to more fully articulate the Great-Books-driven “Great Conversation” and explicit Socratic/seminar pedagogy in upper grades, which would push certain categories toward a fuller classical standard.

  - The curriculum is described as theology-informed and covenantal, with an explicit Christian framework guiding instruction.

These align with Positive Keywords in the Six Elements lexicon (theology, sacred tradition, logos, virtue aligning with a Christian metaphysical frame).

  - The materials clearly ground education in a Christian metaphysical frame, with theology as a governing lens and explicit confessional theology. This aligns with the Positive Indicators for Grounded Theology, liturgical vitality (communion/worship implied through covenantal language), and biblical worldview.

Citations: Greyfriars’ Christian identity and theological grounding are described in multiple pages, including “What is Greyfriars?” and the Statements of Faith, with a covenantal and Westminster-based theological stance.  

### Category 2: School Purpose and Goal — CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - Mission/goal statements emphasize formation of virtue and wisdom, shaping “intelligent thinkers” who think God’s thoughts after Him and aim to transform future generations for the Kingdom. There is explicit focus on character and formation, not merely information or career prep. 
  - Wording such as “classical, Christian, covenantal” and “grammar, logic, rhetoric” as a path to formation reflects paideia-style aims beyond mere test scores. 
  - “The goal is for students to present ideas effectively” and the emphasis on a timeless, time-tested education signals formation toward virtue and human excellence. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive keywords present: formation, virtue, wisdom, human flourishing, telos, great conversation, classical mission, moral imagination, reverence, wonder. These map to Positive Keywords in the classical lexicon. 
  - Negative keywords: not observed (the site does not emphasize purely vocational training or workforce-readiness at the expense of formation).
- Why this rating
  - The school foregrounds formation and the pursuit of truth, goodness, and beauty in a way that aligns with classical aims of character formation and wisdom, rather than exclusively job-readiness.

Citations: Mission/goal language and formation emphasis are explicit in the admissions/educational philosophy sections and the mission statements. 

### Category 3: Curriculum — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - The public materials emphasize the Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and the broader liberal-arts trajectory, with a history-literature-languages-science-math-Spanish-Greek-Philosophy-and-theology lineup. The explicit focus on grammar/logic/rhetoric is present; Great Books/Great Conversation and explicit Great-Books requirements are not clearly stated on the site, though “Great Books” messaging appears in the classical education lexicon rather than as a clear site promise.  
  - The curriculum listing includes History and Philosophy, Literature, Math, Science, Language (Greek/Spanish), Logic & Rhetoric, Theology, and Aesthetics; this aligns with the basic liberal-arts framework and Trivium pedagogy, though the Great Books/Great Conversation emphasis is not explicit in the site copy. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive indicators present: Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric), history/philosophy, literature, languages, theology, aesthetics. This matches core elements of the “Seven Liberal Arts” and the traditional Trivium emphasis. 
  - Negative indicators present: absence of explicit Great Books/Great Conversation language or primary-source canonical reading on the public site; no explicit quadrivium/mathematics-musica-astronomy integration described publicly.
- Why this rating
  - The curriculum clearly centers around the Trivium and liberal-arts structure, which is a hallmark of classical education. However, the absence of explicit Great Books/primary-text canon emphasis in the site copy (as opposed to what a full classical program with Great Books would require) means it does not fully meet the canonical “Great Books/Great Conversation” criterion in the public-facing materials. This yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rating for the category.

Citations: Curriculum statements and subject list appear in the site copy; Great Books/Great Conversation language is present in classical education literature but not explicitly promised on the site. 

### Category 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - The site describes a university-model class schedule (three days on campus, two days at home), which is a distinctive pedagogical structure, and a focus on grammar/logic/rhetoric as core. This points to structured, discipline-based pedagogy rather than a purely modern, project-based approach. 
  - There is emphasis on the educated rhetoric, with opportunities for students to present and engage in argument (rhetoric focus is named in curriculum). However, explicit Socratic seminars, seminars, or debates, and a clearly articulated progressive sequence (Socratic pedagogy in upper grades) are not heavily evidenced in the public copy. 
  - Some elements described in classical literature about pedagogy (scolè, contemplative/restful learning, memory-chanting in some stages) are not explicitly described in the public site materials; those elements appear in the classical-rubric documents, not in the direct site copy. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive indicators present: rhetoric training, grammar/logic/rhetoric, disciplined memory-related practices (implied by Trivium orientation and disciplined schedule), formal structure (university-model). These map to several Positive Pedagogy keywords in the Six Elements lexicon (Socratic dialogue, rhetorical training, seminars, etc., are relevant, though the explicit Socratic/seminar language is not strongly evidenced on the site). 
  - Negative indicators present: lack of explicit Socratic seminars, explicit memory-chanting, or scholé days language on the site; absence of explicit project-based or experiential-learning emphasis in the public copy. The Six Elements negative keywords list such patterns as warnings for not fully classical pedagogy.
- Why this rating
  - Pedagogical methods shown (university-model, emphasis on Trivium) demonstrate classical practices, but the lack of explicit Socratic/seminar and deeper contemplative/restful pedagogy descriptions in the public materials suggests the pedagogy is not yet fully aligned with the strongest classical pedagogy signals in the rubric.

Citations: University-model schedule and Trivium emphasis appear in the public copy; Socratic/seminar pedagogy is not strongly evidenced in the site copy.

## What Would Move the Needle

- For Category 3 (Curriculum): Make explicit the Great Books/Great Conversation component in public-facing materials, with a public statement about primary texts, canonical authors, and cross-disciplinary Great-Book integration. Also consider clarifying Quadrivium components if included (e.g., music/geometry/arithmetic) to signal full liberal-arts breadth.
- For Category 4 (Pedagogy): Explicitly describe Socratic/seminar-based practices in upper grades, and include a clear articulation of scholé/restful learning practices (memory work, recitation, chanting) as part of daily pedagogy, aligning with classical pedagogy signals.
- For Category 6 (Parents & Community): Expand communications to include more explicit parent-formation opportunities (book groups, parent seminars on classical pedagogy, liturgical rhythms in the home) and more routine open-campus events that invite ongoing parental formation beyond admission cycles.
