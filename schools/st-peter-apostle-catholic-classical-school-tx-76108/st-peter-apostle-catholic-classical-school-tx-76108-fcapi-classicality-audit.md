## Executive Summary

St Peter the Apostle Catholic Classical School in Fort Worth, TX (76108) presents a clearly articulated Catholic classical identity with a structured program that aligns closely with many classical education ideals. Public materials emphasize a Catholic, transcendentally oriented framework (truth, goodness, beauty) integrated through a theology-informed approach, liturgical life, and a mission centered on formation in virtue. There is explicit mention of Catholic formation, weekly Mass and daily religion, and a philosophy of education that treats each student as a person created for virtue and growth. At the same time, certain core classical features—most notably explicit Great Books / Great Conversation emphasis and explicit Socratic pedagogy across the upper grades—are less plainly documented on the school’s public-facing materials. The aggregate picture places the school at SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall, with strong evidence in Religious Foundations, Purpose/Goal, and Community, and more tentative alignment in Curriculum and Pedagogy, and uncertain or partial signals for Faculty. The school demonstrates ongoing commitment to classical identity (adoption of a Classical Education model in 2019 and accreditation through Classical Latin School Association in 2023) and broad parental engagement through liturgical life and parish affiliation.

Evidence-informed snapshot:
- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations are strongly represented: explicit statements of truth, beauty, goodness as transcendentals; Catholic theology and liturgical life (Adoration, Mass, daily prayer) frame the school’s identity. 
- School Purpose and Goal show formation in virtue and wisdom as core aims, with language about growing in virtue and forming the whole person in a Catholic framework. 
- Curriculum signals include grammar, logic, Latin, and Western Civilization within a Catholic-classical framework, plus explicit integration of Catholic values across subjects; however, explicit Great Books/Great Conversation language is not clearly documented in public materials. 
- Pedagogy signals are less explicit about Socratic seminars or scholé-like practices in public materials, though the program is described as classical and philosophically oriented; explicit references to classical pedagogy terms are not uniformly evident on the site. 
- Faculty signals suggest a traditional, virtue-centered conception of teachers (the public materials emphasize formation, mission, and virtue in education), but explicit “living curriculum” or faculty portraits are not clearly spelled out in the available public pages. 
- Parents & Community signals show clear partnership with families and parochial emphasis, with open parental involvement through volunteering, admission processes, and parish connections; the school frames parents as co-educators in a Catholic classical ethos.

  - The school’s theology-informed philosophy positions teachers and students within a Catholic framework and emphasizes formation through faith and reason. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis

  - Negative: few explicit signals of secular relativism or values-neutral ethics in the public materials.
  - Quantitative note: Positive indicators appear multiple times (roughly 6–8 distinctive terms across statements); negative indicators are scarce or absent in public materials.
- Why this rating
  - The school clearly anchors education in transcendentals (Truth, Goodness, Beauty) and embeds Catholic theology and liturgical practice into the educational experience, consistent with classical expectations for religious and metaphysical foundations. 
  - The level of explicit liturgical life and catechetical emphasis in public materials supports a classical religious basis, though some classical signals (e.g., scholastic pedagogy terms) are less visible in the site copy. 

### Element 2: School Purpose and Goal — CLASSICAL

### Element 3: School Curriculum — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

- Evidence
  - The curriculum includes grammar, logic (School of Logic in the lower-to-middle grades), and Latin, with Western Civilization and American/Modern Studies mentioned in the program. This aligns with the Trivium-based structure and liberal arts emphasis. 
  - The site states that Catholic classical education infuses the entire curriculum and emphasizes a unified, value-infused approach to learning. 
  - Great Books / Great Conversation is not explicitly named in public materials; the public materials emphasize Western civilization, Catholic formation, and integration rather than a stated program of Great Books or a formal Great Conversation as core curriculum. The absence of explicit Great Books language is a notable gap for a fully classical signal in the curriculum. 
- Positive/Negative keyword analysis
  - Positive: Seven Liberal Arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), Latin, Western Civilization, history-focused content, integration of faith with curriculum.
  - Negative: No explicit Great Books / Great Conversation language; potential lack of explicit emphasis on the complete Form of the Liberal Arts beyond grammar/logic/Latin in visible materials.
  - Quantitative note: Positive cues are present (4–5 distinct terms/claims). Negative cues (explicit Great Books/Great Conversation and full quadrivium references) are not evident in public pages.
- Why this rating
  - The presence of grammar, logic, Latin, and Western Civilization demonstrates solid classical strands, but the absence of explicit Great Books/Great Conversation and full quadrivium signals space for stronger classical articulation. This yields a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rating.

- Positive/Negative keyword analysis

  - Negative: explicit references to Socratic discussions, Scholé days, or a full spectrum of classical pedagogy terms are not clearly documented on the site.
  - Quantitative note: Positive indicators are limited in the public site copy; negatives are not clearly stated but explicit pedagogy keywords (Socratic seminars, scholé) are not strongly visible.
- Why this rating
  - The school’s Catholic-classical framing supports a significant classical pedagogy orientation, but the public documentation does not consistently enumerate or demonstrate the full spectrum of classical teaching methods (e.g., sustained Socratic practice, scholé rhythms, or explicit memorization/recitation traditions). This places Pedagogy at SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL.

What Would Move the Needle

- Make Great Books / Great Conversation explicit in Curriculum: Publicly articulate a Great Books component or “Great Conversation” tradition within the integrated Western Civilization framework and identify a core list of primary sources or canonical texts to be studied in upper grades. This would strengthen Element 3 and push toward CLASSICAL.
- Expand explicit Pedagogy signals: Publicly describe the specific teaching methods (e.g., Socratic seminars, scholé days, memorization/recitation, and rhetoric/declamation opportunities) and provide examples of how these are implemented in K–12; this would strengthen Element 4.
- Heighten Faculty signals: Highlight the living-curriculum concept through explicit statements about faculty as mentors, role models, and co-learners in a classical sense; display portraits or profiles that connect teacher formation with student formation. This would strengthen Element 5.
- Deepen Great-Conversation-oriented assessment: Publicly delineate assessment methods that align with classical pedagogy (narration, copywork, memorization, and disciplined oral presentations) to reinforce Element 3.
- Continue and publicize parent-formation initiatives: Build and publicize structured parent-education events (book studies, lectures, liturgical year activities) and a clear cadence of family formation activities to sustain and expand Element 6.
