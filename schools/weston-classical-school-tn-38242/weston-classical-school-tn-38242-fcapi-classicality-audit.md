## Executive Summary

Weston Classical School, Paris, TN (Weston Classical School TN 38242) presents a robust Christian, classical orientation with a clear emphasis on formation of character, virtue, and a Western, classical intellectual heritage. Public-facing materials stress a Christian worldview weaved through a classical education, with explicit emphasis on Latin, history, literature, and a formation-focused mission. While the school's materials demonstrate strong religious and formation-oriented aims, there is less explicit public articulation of certain core classical elements (notably the explicit triad of grammar–logic–rhetoric, and the Great Books/Great Conversation framing), which tempers a full, textbook Classical rating in a couple of categories.

Overall assessment: CLASSICAL. The school aligns strongly with Religious and Metaphysical Foundations, School Purpose and Goal, and Faculty, while Curriculum and Pedagogy show clear classical influence but lack explicit articulation of all six canonical elements. Parents & Community practices are present but not as fully developed as a canonical Classical School would require. The aggregate picture supports Weston Classical School as CLASSICAL with room for needle-moving steps (outlined below) to achieve full canonical alignment.

What would move the needle is to articulate and enact: explicit Great Books/Great Conversation language and materials; an explicit Trivium-focused curriculum (grammar/logic/rhetoric) emphasis; a formal plan for parent partnership as co-educators; and a documented emphasis on Socratic pedagogy and a more explicit "teacher as living curriculum" framing across all grades.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Pedagogical language highlights: immersion in Latin and living-language instruction; the curriculum uses a bilingual Latin-English approach and an emphasis on inline language acquisition and classical method. There are references to “active spoken Latin” and immersive methods. 
- The public materials emphasize formation, contemplation, and memory through language study and literature, with a motto “non scholae sed vitae discimus” indicating a philosophically contemplative stance. There is no explicit, widespread articulation of Socratic seminars, debates, or formal rhetorical training across all grades in the public content. The presence of Latin immersion and living-language pedagogy signals Classical methods, but the absence of explicit Socratic/Semina language in public content suggests the pedagogy is not fully enumerated in canonical terms. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: Scholé (restful/ contemplative learning) is mentioned in the lexicon but not publicly as a formal term in Weston’s site; the site does emphasize contemplative formation through reading and language. Positive indicators such as contemplation, wonder, and memory work may be inferred from “non scholae sed vitae” and Latin immersion. The lexicon’s Pedagogy indicators (Socratic dialogue, seminars, rhetoric, memorization/recitation, Scholé) align with some of Weston’s approaches, but explicit, widespread usage of these traditional pedagogy forms is not clearly enumerated in Weston’s public content. 
- Negative keywords present: the public content does not emphasize project-based or strictly experiential, student-led pedagogy to the exclusion of contemplative methods; it instead foregrounds language immersion and classical formation. The lexicon warns against a narrow, non-dialogic approach; Weston’s public materials do not clearly demonstrate a fully dialogic or purely project-based pedagogy.

Why this rating
- Weston demonstrates foundational classical pedagogy through Latin immersion and a formation-first approach, but lacks explicit public articulation of the full canonical pedagogy set (Socratic seminars, Scholé-based contemplative rhythms, and memorization/recitation as a formal, cross-grade practice). As such, the Pedagogy category sits at SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Citations: Public statements about Latin immersion and the “non scholae sed vitae discimus” motto; references to chaplain-led chapel and language-based instruction.

## What Would Move the Needle

- Curriculum: Publicly articulate and implement the full liberal-arts framework (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and explicitly reference Great Books/Great Conversation within the curriculum description. Add concrete Great Books-based courses or reading lists and a declared plan for the Great Conversation across grades.
- Pedagogy: Publicly commit to explicit Socratic/seminal seminar pedagogies, with a cross-grade, documented approach (e.g., Harkness-style dialogue, regular seminars, and rhetoric training). Emphasize Scholé/restful learning rhythms and memorization/recitation as formal, cross-year practices.
- Parents & Community: Establish explicit, public-facing programs that treat parents as co-teachers and partners in classical education (parent formation sessions, book studies, catechesis for families, parent visitation policies that clearly articulate engagement expectations).
- Curriculum content: Expand language and text choices to explicitly include classical tracts and primary sources, with a public plan showing how Latin, Greek (if applicable), and foundational texts interconnect with history and philosophy in cross-curricular threads.
- Community formation: Formalize liturgical rhythms and communal practices as a regular, integrated part of the school week with documented rituals that align with the liturgical calendar, theology, and sacred arts (as appropriate).

Citations used to inform these needle-moving ideas are rooted in the Six Essential Elements lexicon and its explicit calls for grammar/logic/rhetoric, Great Books/Great Conversation, Scholé, and parent partnership.
