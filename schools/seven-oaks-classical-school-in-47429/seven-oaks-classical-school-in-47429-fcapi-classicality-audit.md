## Executive Summary

Seven Oaks Classical School (Ellettsville, IN 47429) presents a deliberately classical public charter model with a strong emphasis on virtue, character formation, and a liberal-arts curriculum. Publicly marketed as a school of choice with a focus on “Whole-Child Formation” and a Hillsdale College aligned K-12 program, the school declares adherence to classical ideals while explicitly stating it is not a religious school. This combination yields a mixed classical signal: substantively classical in goal and culture, yet lacking in explicit theological or liturgical programming. Overall, the school earns a SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL designation, with notable strengths in formation, virtue, and classical curricular aims, but lacking explicit, publicly stated doctrinal or liturgical foundations and some traditional pedagogy signals (e.g., explicit all-lane Socratic inquiry and Great-Books-anchored Great Conversation as campus-wide practice).

Key insights:
- Public charter status with Hillsdale-supported, classical K-12 curriculum; strong emphasis on virtue, character, and formation beyond mere information transfer.
- Explicit statements about neutrality on religion, while foregrounding classical aims (truth, goodness, beauty) and moral formation as primary goals.
- Curriculum signals include liberal-arts emphasis, Latin, Western tradition, and a Lyceum program hinting at dialogue between ancient wisdom and modern science; however, explicit Great Books/Great Conversation framing is not consistently stated in public materials.
- Pedagogical signals are partial: some elements suggest contemplative or non-mechanical learning, while explicit Socratic seminars or full upper-school rhetoric training are not consistently documented on public pages.
- Strong faculty signals (Master Teachers; Hillsdale alignment) and robust parent/community engagement posture.

Evidence and category-by-category analysis below uses keyword mapping to the Six Essential Elements of a Classical School, drawing on both the public site content and the knowledge-base rubrics.

- The classical education rationale is framed around transcendent ideals: “A classical education is an education aimed at human excellence... orient students’ minds and affections to the true, the beautiful, and the good.” This aligns with transcendental moral realism in the Six Elements framework. 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: true, beautiful, good (the triad in the “true, the beautiful, and the good” phrase); virtue; character; moral formation; formation; wisdom. These map to the Positive Keywords for Transcendental Truth/Goodness/Beauty and virtue formation in the Six Elements doc. 
- Negative keywords present: explicit religious identity or mission is denied (i.e., not religious). This corresponds to the Negative/guarded signals in the document’s framework about religious grounding. 

Why this rating
- The school foregrounds transcendental ideals (truth, beauty, goodness) but explicitly disavows religious identity and liturgical practice; there is no theology-centered or liturgical dimension publicly claimed. This yields partial alignment with the foundational element—transcendental moral realism is present, but theology and liturgical/community practices are not. The balance tips toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Score justification notes from the Six Elements framework
- The presence of “true, good, beautiful” and “virtue/character formation” supports the transcendental moral realism and formation components, but the absence of theological or liturgical grounding keeps category 1 from CLASSICAL.

Cited sources: classical ends/true-beauty-good phrasing; explicit non-religious stance.

### Element 3: School Curriculum — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Liberal Arts foundation and curriculum scope: “The mission of Seven Oaks Classical School is to train the minds and improve the hearts of young people through a rigorous classical education in the liberal arts and sciences, with instruction in the principles of moral character and civic virtue.” This indicates a liberal-arts curriculum rooted in classical aims. 
- Foundational emphasis on languages, fine arts, and sciences: The site states a strong foundation in languages, fine arts, and sciences, signaling a broad liberal-arts approach. 
- Latin emphasis and Western tradition: The curriculum highlights Latin study and Western cultural literacy, signaling discipline-specific classical strands. 
- The Lyceum program signals an emphasis on conversation between ancient wisdom and modern science (potentially aligning with Great Conversation). 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: liberal arts, languages, fine arts, sciences; classical education; Latin; Western tradition; Great Conversation (via Lyceum concept); Master Teacher program (supporting a classical pedagogy).   
- Negative keywords present: explicit reference to Great Books/Great Conversation as a formal, campus-wide Great Books program is not consistently stated on public pages (though Lyceum hints at classical dialogue). This reduces the strength of the Great Books/Great Conversation signal in public materials. 

Why this rating
- The curriculum is clearly anchored in liberal-arts and classical aims (languages, Western tradition, and a Lyceum-style dialogue concept), but there is less explicit, consistent public-facing language about Great Books as a formal, campus-wide Great Conversation program or a full trivium/quadrivium framing. This partial alignment places Element 3 in SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Cited sources: curriculum overview, language/Latin emphasis, Western tradition, Lyceum.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Public pedagogy signals include a mix of traditional and modern approaches (Hillsdale-aligned K-12 model) and emphasis on forming minds and hearts, not just drilling content. The public materials emphasize disciplined, values-driven learning and a blend of traditional and cutting-edge techniques. 
- The Lyceum and School events indicate opportunities for Socratic-style exchange and dialogue (the Lyceum tagline mentions “a conversation between ancient wisdom and modern science”). This suggests exposure to dialogic pedagogy in some contexts. 
- There is no consistently explicit statement of formal Socratic seminars or upper-school rhetoric training on public pages; the presence of the Hillsdale partnership and specific master-teacher programs implies a strong, teacher-led, standards-based framework rather than a pure Socratic method across all grades. Public pages do not consistently claim “Socratic discussions” as a universal method. The absence of explicit, campus-wide statements about Socratic seminars or debates is notable. 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: contemplative/restful learning signals are not strongly asserted, but “formation” and “character” imply holistic pedagogy; “Socratic” language appears only in specific program contexts (Lyceum) rather than as a campus-wide teaching method. The Great Conversation/socratic-style signals exist but are not uniformly described across grade levels in public materials. 
- Negative keywords present: lack of explicit, school-wide Socratic pedagogy or project-based/experiential-learning signals in public pages; absence of a consistent “Socratic discussion in upper school” claim. 

Why this rating
- The school shows classical alignment in formational aims and occasional dialogic opportunities, but public-facing pedagogy signals are not uniformly explicit (especially compared with explicit Socratic/great-books pedagogy signals in other classical schools). This results in SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Cited sources: Lyceum (dialogue concept), Hillsdale-aligned pedagogy, and absence of universal Socratic claims on public pages.

- School leadership and "Headmaster" visibility: Public pages introduce the Head of School and administration, highlighting a structured leadership model around virtue, discipline, and academic rigor. 
- Public statements of teacher quality and recruitment: The public-facing admissions and staff pages describe a professional, virtuous faculty profile and a system that prizes teacher expertise and character. 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: Master Teacher, Hillsdale K-12, living curriculum, faculty as core, expert teachers, virtuous modeling, headmaster, administration team.  
- Negative keywords present: none detected in public materials (no explicit negative signals about faculty). 

Why this rating
- The explicit articulation of master teachers, Hillsdale-supported faculty development, and a philosophy that the faculty constitutes the living curriculum strongly align with CLASSICAL. Element 5 is CLASSICAL.

Cited sources: Master Teachers, Hillsdale alignment, faculty as living curriculum.

## What Would Move the Needle

- Public articulation of a more explicit Great Books/Great Conversation framework across the full K-12 program (not just the Lyceum/programmatic hints). This would strengthen Element 3 (Curriculum) and Element 4 (Pedagogy) by codifying a universal Great Conversation approach.
- Clear, campus-wide description of Socratic or seminar-based pedagogy in upper grades, including structured opportunities for Socratic dialogue and debates, to strengthen Element 4.
- Explicit integration of Sabbath-like or liturgical/sacred rhythms (even if non-denominational) to bolster Element 1's religious/metaphysical dimension, provided the school intends to maintain its non-religious stance publicly. If a liturgical dimension remains off the table, consider more explicit discussion of how virtue and moral formation are cultivated across disciplines to fortify Element 1 in a non-theological sense.
- A public, documented articulation of the trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and quadrivium in the curriculum, with examples of how those stages appear in K-12 coursework, to enhance Element 3.
- More frequent, public-facing references to the Great Books canon, Great Conversation, and primary source engagement to strengthen Element 3 and align more closely with Six Elements expectations.
