## Executive Summary

Crossville Christian School Tn 38555 (operating as Christian Academy of the Cumberlands on the CAC site) presents a strongly religiously grounded program, with explicit Christian theology, biblical worldview integration, and clear partnership with families. Public-facing materials emphasize Christ-centered formation, biblical instruction, and a partnership with parents; there is explicit mention of a Statement of Faith and a biblically-based mission. However, the school's public materials do not clearly articulate a classical curriculum or pedagogy in the sense described by the classical school rubric (e.g., explicit reference to Great Books, grammar–logic–rhetoric progression, or Socratic inquiry in the upper grades). The curriculum appears to hinge on a Christian worldview and a predefined Christian curriculum (ABEKA) rather than a traditional liberal-arts focus. Taken together, Crossville Christian School Tn 38555 demonstrates clear Classical affinities in religious foundations and parental/community integration, modest Classical alignment in pedagogy and faculty orientation, but relatively weak Classical signal in explicit curriculum structure and high-school Socratic/seminar traditions. Overall, the school is BEST described as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

Key highlights
- Strong Religious and Metaphysical Foundations: explicit theology, biblical worldview, Statement of Faith, and Christ-centered framing permeate mission and program materials.  
- Clear School Purpose aligned with formation and faith integration, though with explicit emphasis on college/career readiness as a current priority in practice. 
- Curriculum oriented around Biblical instruction and Christian worldview (ABEKA-based materials), with limited public emphasis on Great Books or liberal-arts canonical study. 
- Pedagogy appears more traditional/instructional (ABEKA-driven) rather than explicitly Classical (few references to Socratic seminars, great-books pedagogy, or memory-centered scholé in public content). 
- Faculty presence is documented (staff/faculty page, accreditation), but the public materials do not foreground the “living curriculum” or “portrait of teacher” mindset characteristic of some classical schools. 
- Parents and Community are positioned as partners and co-educators, with visible emphasis on home–school alignment and parental involvement. 

What would move the needle: concrete steps to reach CLASSICAL
- Expand curriculum to explicitly include liberal-arts elements: integrate grammar–logic–rhetoric progression (even if in a Christian context), include a canon of classical literature or Great Books selections, and emphasize history as Western civilization through primary texts.
- Adopt explicit classical pedagogy in upper grades: introduce Socratic discussions, seminars, and rhetoric-focused assessment; incorporate memory-driven recitation and disciplined oral expression as formal components.
- Enhance the “living curriculum” and teacher portrait: articulate a formal faculty development plan grounded in classical pedagogy and publish a clearly defined “teacher profile” that aligns with classical formation goals.
- Increase explicit Great Conversation and wonder: add experiences of intellectual awe, cross-disciplinary inquiry, and opportunities for students to engage with enduring questions and texts beyond standard Christian curricula.
- Deepen parent formation and involvement in classical aims: expand book studies, parent seminars, and visible open-campus opportunities to involve families in the Great Conversation and classical formation.

## Executive Summary — Element-by-Element Snapshot

Evidence
- Materials foreground a distinctly Christian framework: biblical worldview, biblical instruction, and Christ-centered education are repeatedly emphasized in mission statements and doctrinal notes. For example, CAC’s mission describes developing students with a Christian worldview through biblically-based teaching, and a Statement of Faith anchors authority to Scripture.  
- Calls to integrate God’s truth across life and learning are explicit, indicating transcendental aims and moral formation. Public materials present a clear theology-centered education rather than a secular epistemology. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive indicators present: truth/true, God, biblical worldview, theology, Word of God, Christ-centered, Scripture, faith-based. Representative matches include “biblical worldview,” “Statement of Faith,” “Word of God,” and “Christ-centered home, church, and school” language. 
- Negative indicators: none evident in public materials (no explicit calls to secular or relativist approaches). The absence of secular framing strengthens the religious foundation signal.

Why this rating
- The school’s public communications strongly affirm theological grounding and communal worship-oriented sensibilities, which align with the Positive keywords for Religious and Metaphysical Foundations. There is limited indication of secular or relativist framing, so the category reads as CLASSICAL-adjacent in its religious/metaphysical orientation, with room to more fully realize the classical elements (e.g., liturgical practices) in public-facing materials.

### Element 2: School Purpose and Goal — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The mission emphasizes forming the whole student within a Christian worldview, with biblically-based teaching and service aims. It calls for equipping the next generation in service to the Lord and others. This aligns with formation and virtue-centered aims rather than purely utilitarian outcomes. 
- The curriculum and mission also mention preparation for college and career, indicating a balancing of formation with workforce/career aims (a modern emphasis that some classical promoters would view as a potential tension). The public materials note the aim to prepare for college and career as part of the program. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive indicators present: formation, virtue, human flourishing, liberal education (conceptual alignment), Christian worldview, classical mission, telos, true/good/beautiful, contemplation (where mentioned), Great Conversation (not explicitly named, but the formation aims reflect similar aims in a Christian frame).
- Negative indicators present: explicit emphasis on college/career preparation noted within the public materials, which can be seen as a modern workforce emphasis that some classical-sector criteria flag as a departure from formation-centric aims. This is evidenced by language about continuing to prepare for college and career. 

Why this rating
- The school prioritizes formation, virtue, and a Christian telos, which aligns with classical aims; however, the public materials also emphasize college/career readiness as a practical outcome, which drags the category toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL. The absence (so far) of explicit Great Conversation framing (canonical canon, classical telos) also tempers the rating.

### Element 3: School Curriculum — NOT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The curriculum is described in terms of Bible, Language Arts, Math, Science, and History, with explicit mention of the ABEKA program as the foundational curricular approach. This signals a Biblically-based Christian curriculum rather than the liberal-arts canon approach typical of classical programs. 
- There is no explicit mention of liberal arts fundamentals (grammar, logic, rhetoric), Great Books, or the Great Conversation, nor of canonical Western texts or Latin/Greek (public-facing materials do not articulate these elements). 
- The school does emphasize a biblical worldview across the curriculum, but the absence of Great Books, classical languages, or a triad of grammar/logic/rhetoric signals a non-classical curricular structure. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive indicators: Bible-based instruction; biblical worldview; foundational Christian curriculum; explicit mention of “biblically-based teaching.” These align with the religious dimension of curriculum but not with classical liberal-arts structure. 
- Negative indicators: Lack of reference to Seven Liberal Arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), Great Books, canonical texts, or the Trivium/Quadrivium framework. There is also no explicit history-integration across disciplines as a Great Books approach. Positive mentions of a Christian curriculum are present, but this is not the same as “classical” curriculum signals.

Why this rating
- Given the strong Christian emphasis but the absence of classical-curriculum markers (Great Books, grammar–logic–rhetoric progression, canonical texts), the category is NOT CLASSICAL according to the strict six-element rubric.

### Element 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — NOT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Public materials describe an ABEKA-based curriculum and mention live instruction and discussion in some classes, often with video-based instruction in others. This suggests a structured, teacher-led program rather than a distinctly Classical pedagogy built on Socratic seminars, memory work, and Scholé practices. 
- There is no explicit public articulation of Socratic seminars, classical memory work, or rhetoric training in the public-facing materials. The absence of these classical pedagogical markers (Socratic dialogue, memorization via dictation, or regular Seminars) supports a non-classical pedagogy reading. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive indicators: Some mention of live instruction and discussion in some classes; a structured, teacher-led approach. These align with traditional pedagogy but not with classical pedagogy per se (which would emphasize memory work, recitation, and Socratic dialogue more explicitly).
- Negative indicators: Absence of core Classical pedagogical signals in the public content: Scholé (restful learning), memory work, recitation, chanting, Socratic seminars, Paideia-style dialogue, or upper-grade rhetoric track. The classical lexicon identifies these as hallmark practices, which are not evident here. 

Why this rating
- The school demonstrates a structured, faith-integrated pedagogy but lacks explicit classical teaching methods in public materials. Therefore, NOT CLASSICAL.

Why this rating

### Element 6: School Parents and Community — CLASSICAL

This aligns with the classical emphasis on parental cooperation and formation of the whole family. 
- The materials discuss a Christ-centered home–church–school network and welcome parental engagement and visits, reflecting the “parents as co-teachers” and “open campus” ethos that classical models often promote. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive indicators present: partnership with parents, parental formation/education opportunities (handbooks, seminars implied by parent formation), open-campus-friendly language (visits).
- Negative indicators: None evident in public communications; no minimal-parent involvement described as a consumer model; rather, the materials frame parents as co-educators.

Why this rating
- The school clearly prioritizes a collaborative parental role and family engagement, matching classical signals for Parents & Community.

## What Would Move the Needle

- Make explicit the classical curriculum elements: publish and implement a formal plan that foregrounds grammar–logic–rhetoric progression, Great Books or canonical texts, and a clear Trivium/Quadrivium orientation within the curriculum.
- Adopt explicit Classical Pedagogy: incorporate Socratic seminars, disciplined memory work or recitations, and scholé-informed pauses; articulate a clear upper-grade rhetoric/declamation pathway.
- Elevate the living-curriculum concept: publish a formal teacher-profile/portrait, faculty formation plans, and visible demonstrations of teacher-led classical practice in classrooms.
- Deepen Great Conversation and wonder: weave regular cross-disciplinary inquiries into major topics, promoting wonder and awe in learning.
- Expand parent formation to classical lines: widen book-studies for parents, host regular classical-education seminars, and create structured opportunities for parent involvement in canonical reading and discussions.
