## Executive Summary

Tulsa Classical Academy (TCA) presents a robust classical framework, with strong alignment in Curriculum and a communicative emphasis on virtue, wisdom, and the Western intellectual tradition. The public materials repeatedly foreground a Hillsdale College K-12 curriculum, explicit attention to liberal arts, classical literature, and the cultivation of character and civic virtue. Evidence from the school’s site highlights emphasis on truth-goodness-beauty in learning, a Western canon orientation (e.g., Aristotle, The Federalist Papers, The US Constitution), and a mission to form humane, thoughtful citizens, not merely to job-skill students. These elements anchor TCA firmly in a Classical orientation, particularly in Curriculum and Community. However, explicit liturgical/theological practices and some facets of pedagogy (e.g., Socratic seminars, Scholé/restful learning, and explicit memorization/recitation practices) are less clearly documented on public-facing materials, signaling room for deeper alignment in those areas.

Category-by-category synthesis (based on keyword indicators drawn from the Six Essential Elements framework and the school’s published materials):

- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Purpose and Goal: CLASSICAL
- Curriculum: CLASSICAL
- Pedagogy: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Faculty: CLASSICAL
- Parents & Community: CLASSICAL

Executive-Category takeaways emphasize a generally classical profile with strong curricular/human-formation signals, but with fewer explicit liturgical/theological signals and some pedagogy signals that could be reinforced to achieve a fuller classical cadence across all six elements.

Citations drawing on both the school’s public materials and the classical rubric are embedded in the Evidence sections for each category, and the overall assessment uses the Six Essential Elements as the grounding rubric.

## Executive Summary — Element-by-Element Snapshot

### Element 1: Religious and Metaphysical Foundations — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Tulsa Classical Academy presents itself as an American classical charter school with a focus on virtue, wisdom, and wonder, and explicitly frames its program as grounded in a Western liberal-arts tradition. The site emphasizes character formation and virtue as core aims and references moral formation and the good life as overarching objectives (e.g., “An American Classical Education,” “Character Formation,” and “Virtue, Wisdom, Wonder”). 
- The Hillsdale K-12 Curriculum is highlighted as the foundational approach, signaling alignment with a theologically inclined, transcendentally grounded classical methodology (though not explicitly stated as theology on all pages). This is evidenced by “HILLSDALE K-12 CURRICULUM” and its emphasis on literacy, classical literature, and core knowledge. 
- The public-facing materials foreground the Good, True, and Beautiful as a learning aim in classrooms (e.g., “Wonder—Every classroom is filled with curiosity and exploration of the Good, True, and Beautiful”). This reflects transcendental aims consistent with classical education’s moral realism. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: virtue, wisdom, wonder, moral formation, life of the mind, good/true/beautiful, moral character.
- Negative keywords: not clearly present in the public materials (no explicit emphasis on secular relativism, values-neutral ethics, or relativistic frameworks). The absence of overt secular-humanist or relativist signals strengthens alignment with transcendental/moral realism signals in the public materials.

Why this rating
- The presence of explicit virtue-related language, calls to moral formation, and the Good-True-Beautiful triad signals alignment with Transcendental Moral Realism and Formation. However, explicit liturgical/communal practices or direct theological content are not consistently documented in public-facing materials, which tempers a full liturgical/theological articulation. The Hillsdale/K-12 curriculum linkage implies theological underpinnings in the broader classical framework, but the site stops short of explicit liturgy or doctrinal articulation in the public pages. Therefore, the category is BEST described as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL.

Citations
- Good, True, Beautiful emphasis and virtue/wisdom language: Tulsa site (Tulsa Classical Academy) – “Classrooms inspire virtue, wisdom, and wonder.” 
- Hillsdale K-12 curriculum framing: Tulsa site – “HILLSDALE K-12 CURRICULUM” and explicit curriculum components. 
- Western-tradition and moral formation signals: Curriculum emphasis and “Character Formation.”

Why this rating
- The public materials emphasize formation and moral development over mere information or job preparation, aligning with classical aims of formation toward human excellence and the enduring truths of virtue and wisdom. The overall purpose signals are clearly Classical.

Citations

### Element 3: School Curriculum — CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The curriculum is described as Hillsdale College K-12 Curriculum, explicitly positioned as a strong liberal-arts-and-sciences program focusing on literacy, explicit phonics, and classical literature. This is a hallmark of classical curriculum design. 
- Core components include reading/writing in cursive, Latin roots beginning in lower grades, and four years of Latin in secondary school, indicating a traditional classical core rather than a purely skills-based approach.  
- The Western-Civilization-centered Cultural Literacy focus and engagement with primary sources such as Aristotle, The Federalist Papers, The US Declaration, US Constitution, and Tocqueville signal direct Great Books/primary-source engagement and a “Great Conversation” orientation. 
- Explicit mention of classical structure (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and a curriculum map featuring moral/political philosophy and government content aligns with the Trivium preparation and liberal-arts emphasis. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: Seven Liberal Arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), Great Books/The Great Conversation, Western civilization, moral/political philosophy, Latin/Greek roots, classical canons, cultural literacy, primary sources.
- Negative keywords present: minimal. No explicit project-based-dominant or social-studies-first framing in the public materials; instead the materials emphasize classical canon and liberal-arts content.

Why this rating
- The curriculum is clearly anchored in classical content and methods (grammar/logical/rhetoric; Great Books; Western canon; Latin; moral/political philosophy). The presence of primary-source engagement and a canon-focused curriculum supports a CLASSICAL rating.

Citations
- Hillsdale K-12 Curriculum framing and explicit core components: Tulsa site. 
- Latin and classical-language progression; history/music linkage; Western-tradition emphasis: Curriculum content. 
- Primary-source exemplars and Great Books orientation (Aristotle, Federalist Papers, US Constitution, Tocqueville): Curriculum map excerpt. 

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The school profile includes a House System designed to foster community, mentorship, virtue, and leadership. This indicates a classical ethos of character formation through structured peer mentorship and guided community life, consistent with a portion of classical pedagogy. 
- Public materials emphasize character formation, civic virtue, and life-long learning, which imply pedagogy oriented toward formation beyond rote information. 
- There is public emphasis on teacher leadership and a “living curriculum” through faculty leadership and mentorship signals, though direct textual references to specific classical methods (Socratic dialogue, Scholé, memorization/recitation, or formal rhetoric instruction) are not consistently spelled out on public pages. The site does reference a Hillsdale K-12 approach, which in classical education often includes Socratic and dialectical elements, but explicit public-page claims are less clear. 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords present: Socratic dialogue (explicitly part of the classical pedagogy lexicon in the rubrics), Scholé (restful learning), memorization/recitation, formation through imitation and practice, rhetoric development, great conversation. The public site does not consistently call out these pedagogical methods in detail, but the Hillsdale/K-12 framing and the emphasis on classical pedagogy imply these methods, and the school’s internal materials (House System, leadership, and moral formation cues) align with these ideas.
- Negative keywords present: Explicit, widespread emphasis on project-based learning dominance or purely experiential learning is not present; the public materials do not foreground heavy project-based instruction as a dominant mode.

Why this rating
- The public-facing materials show substantial alignment with classical pedagogy through character formation and classical-cultural structures, but explicit, recurrent public confirmation of core pedagogical modalities (Socratic seminars, Scholé-based restfulness, chant/memorization recitation, and formal rhetoric training) is not uniformly documented across pages. Therefore, SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL is a cautious, evidence-based rating for Pedagogy.

Citations
- House System as a signaling pedagogical structure aligned with mentorship and virtue: Tulsa site. 
- Character formation and life-long learning signaling formation-focused pedagogy: Tulsa site. 
- Hillsdale/K-12 framing and explicit classical approach: Tulsa site.

## What Would Move the Needle

- Element 1 (Religious and Metaphysical Foundations): Explicit articulation of liturgical or communal spiritual practices and a more explicit theological framing within the public materials would move the needle toward CLASSICAL in this category. Concrete signals such as daily or seasonal liturgical rhythms, chapel/morning prayer, or explicit theological commitments would strengthen transcendental/metaphysical grounding.
- Element 4 (Pedagogy): More explicit public-facing articulation of core pedagogical modalities (Socratic dialogue, Scholé/restful learning, structured memory work/recitation, and overt rhetoric training) would push this category to CLASSICAL. Publishing a concise pedagogy charter or framework that names specific practices (e.g., weekly Socratic seminars, memory work sequences, and structured rhetoric opportunities) would be impactful.
- Other categories (2, 3, 5, 6) already show robust CLASSICAL signals in public materials; minor enhancements (e.g., explicit “Great Books” reading lists, direct references to “Great Conversation,” or a public-facing glossary of classical pedagogy terms) could further reinforce the Classical alignment without changing fundamental orientation.
