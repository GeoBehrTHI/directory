EXECUTIVE SUMMARY — CATEGORY-BY-CATEGORY SNAPSHOT

Element 1: Religious and Metaphysical Foundations — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- The school’s mission/vision emphasizes virtuous character and a search for truth, goodness, and beauty as real ideals. The language points to transcendentals and moral realism rather than secular or relativistic aims.
- Statements tying education to formation, virtue, and moral aims are foregrounded in the mission/vision materials and other public communications.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: truth, goodness, beauty; virtue; moral character; wisdom. Negative keywords (relativism, secular-humanist tendencies) are not foregrounded in MCA materials.
- Frequency estimate: High (multiple explicit references across mission/vision and curriculum content).

Why this rating

- MCA treats truth, goodness, and beauty as real aims, with formation and virtue as central. While explicit liturgical practices are not foregrounded, the transcendental moral-realism framing is strong.

Element 2: School Purpose and Goal — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- The mission/vision describe formation of virtuous citizens and the aim of pursuing truth, goodness, and beauty. The purpose is formation of human excellence and character, not merely job-readiness.
- The school positions education as formation (paideia/scholé) and aims at human flourishing.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: formation, virtue, wisdom, liberal education, telos, great conversation, moral imagination.
- Negative keywords: none prominent in core public statements (no strong emphasis on workforce-focused or 21st-century-workforce language in core statements).

Why this rating

- MCA’s stated aims emphasize formation and human excellence, consistent with classical aims and not primarily job-prep.

Element 3: Curriculum — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- Explicit reference to Trivium (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and Quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy).
- Public materials describe humane letters, parallel English/history in the Rhetoric School, and integrated study of literature, history, philosophy, etc. Great Books/Great Conversations are foregrounded.
- Use of paideia seminars and an integrated, cross-disciplinary approach (humanities/science; cross-curricular) is described.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: Trivium, Quadrivium, Great Books, Great Conversation, humane letters, paideia, scholé, interdisciplinary/harmony of subjects, wonder.
- Negative keywords: some modern “project-based” or “experiential” phrasing appears in other contexts; however, the core curriculum materials emphasize classical content and inquiry rather than modern pedagogy-only approaches.

Why this rating

- The curriculum centers on classical liberal arts and Great Books, with explicit references to the Trivium/Quadrivium and humane letters, meeting the core curriculum criteria for CLASSICAL.

Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- Upper-level pedagogy includes Socratic discussion, seminars, and rhetoric training; paideia/seminars are used in the high school.
- Lower-level pedagogy includes contemplative/restful elements (Scholé), memory work, singing/chanting to deepen memory; gradual shift to Socratic/seminar approaches in higher grades.
- The school describes the progression from Grammar to Dialectic to Rhetoric within a classical framework.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: Socratic, seminar, dialectic, rhetoric, paideia, scholé, memory work, singing/chanting, contemplative learning.
- Negative keywords: minimal, as the public materials emphasize classical pedagogy; any “experiential-only” or purely project-based approach is not the core emphasis.

Why this rating

- MCA explicitly uses Socratic/seminar methods in the upper grades and paideia practice, with restorative/memory-based pedagogy in the lower grades, consistent with CLASSICAL pedagogy.

Element 5: Faculty — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- The materials describe the faculty as the living curriculum and as mentors delivering the classical curriculum; Exploratory Courses are taught by teachers with passions and specializations.
- The visible public materials emphasize teacher-led instruction, mentorship, and teacher-centered delivery of the classical curriculum.
- A portrait of what a teacher should be (in the classical lexicon) is aligned with the culture MCA describes (living curriculum and mentors).

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: living curriculum, mentor, master, teacher as exemplar, classical pedagogy, great-books seminars for faculty.
- Negative keywords: None prominent in core public materials.

Why this rating

- MCA frames faculty as the core drivers of the classical education, delivering the curriculum and guiding students through the Trivium/Quadrivium and Great Books.

Element 6: Parents & Community — CLASSICAL

Evidence

- The school emphasizes partnership with families, co-teaching roles for parents, and parent involvement in governance and school life.
- Public materials reference parental education/gatherings, book studies, and opportunities to visit the campus; the school positions parents as co-educators.
- The school highlights community involvement, field trips, and engagement with leaders and organizations in the local area.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis

- Positive keywords present: partnership with families, co-teachers, parental engagement, family learning, home-school collaboration, community involvement, open campus.
- Negative keywords: none prominent in core public materials (no strong consumer-language framing; rather, partnership language).

Why this rating

- MCA presents a robust classical-parent/community partnership, with explicit mechanisms for parent involvement and ongoing community engagement.

What Would Move the Needle

- No needle-moving changes are required given the CLASSICAL status across all categories. If desired, incremental enhancements could include stronger public articulation of Great Books read-aloud traditions, explicit liturgical rhythms (within a non-sectarian framework), or expanded portraits of the teaching philosophy to further illuminate the “living curriculum” concept for external audiences.
