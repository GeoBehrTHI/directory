## Executive Summary

Our Savior Lutheran School (OSLS) in Grand Rapids, MI clearly presents a classical Christian program anchored in Lutheran catechesis. Across six core categories, OSLS demonstrates core classical commitments: a theology-centered foundation with liturgical practices; formation of virtue and wisdom as the telos of education; a curriculum built on the liberal arts, Great Books, and Western civilization; pedagogy that blends contemplative/restful approaches with Socratic dialogue, rhetoric, and memorization; a faculty culture oriented toward virtue and mentorship; and a robust partnership with parents and an open, family-friendly community orientation. The materials consistently emphasize truth, goodness, and beauty as real and knowable, the centrality of Scripture, and the aim of forming a virtuous, well-educated human being rather than simply training for work. The Great Books and liberal-arts framing, combined with liturgical practice and explicit formation aims, align OSLS with a classical rubric.

Overall classification: CLASSICAL.

- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations: OSLS grounds its program in Scripture and Lutheran confession, with daily worship and catechesis, and a liturgical, communal rhythm. Evidence: explicit statements about the Word of God shaping daily instruction, worship, catechetical recitations, Lutheran hymnody, and centrality of Scripture.  
- Purpose and Goal: The school foregrounds formation, virtue, wisdom, and enduring truths (truth, goodness, beauty) over mere job-readiness. Evidence: formation-focused language, emphasis on virtue and wisdom, and Great-Books framing.  
- Curriculum: Liberal arts core (grammar, logic, rhetoric) plus Great Books and Western civilization; history across the curriculum; emphasis on wonder. Evidence: explicit listing of the seven liberal arts; Great Books; reading and analysis of literature; pursuit of wonder.  
- Pedagogy: Blends contemplative/restful learning with Socratic discussion, memory work, and rhetoric; lower grades include singing/chanting; upper grades incorporate seminars and oratory. Evidence: Socratic questioning; memory/recitation; rhetoric training; singing/chapel integration.  
- Faculty: Implied model-teacher/mentorship orientation with emphasis on virtue and formation; teachers as partners with parents; emphasis on “living curriculum.” Evidence: language about forming students through virtuous teaching; collaboration with parents; emphasis on virtue in teacher-student interactions. 
- Parents & Community: Explicit partnership with parents; openness to visits; parental formation opportunities; emphasis on parents as co-partners in formation. Evidence: “work closely with parents,” “Visit Us” information, invitations for family engagement.

### Element 4: Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Emphasis on contemplative/restful learning (Scholé) and memorization/recitation; lower-school singing and memory work; regular review and chant as memory-building practices. 
- Upper-school pedagogy includes Socratic discussion, seminars, debates, and opportunities to develop rhetoric through speeches. 
- The school notes that some education should be contemplative and restful, while upper grades engage in rigorous dialectic and rhetorical training. 

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive keywords present: Scholé, Socratic dialogue, dialectic, seminars, rhetoric, memorization/recitation, chanting, oral discourse, great conversation, formation through imitation.
- Negative keywords present: none noted (no emphasis on purely project-based or student-directed constructivism that would signal departure from classical pedagogy).

Why this rating
- OSLS demonstrates a dual-track pedagogy: restful, memory-based practices in earlier grades and Socratic/rhetorical methods in later grades, consistent with classical pedagogy models.

What Would Move the Needle

- Given the CLASSICAL classification across all six categories, no major needle-moving changes are required. Minor enhancements could include explicitly naming the “living curriculum” concept in the public materials for Faculty (to reinforce the living-teacher ethos) and adding a concise, school-wide articulation of "parent as co-teacher" language in admissions and about pages to tighten the alignment with classical-parent partnership vocabulary. These would be clarifications rather than policy shifts.
