## Executive Summary

Sundial Classical Farmstead (SCF) presents as a clearly classical-leaning institution with a strong religious-metaphysical orientation, a vocally stated mission of forming virtuous people through beauty, virtue, and truth, and a curriculum and pedagogy aligned with liberal-arts formation, Great Books/Great Conversation sensibilities, and a heritage of contemplative learning. The site materials repeatedly emphasize character formation, virtue, wisdom, and beauty, along with explicit references to Great Conversation, living books, narration, and memorization. There is also a defined Christian/Orthodox catechesis dimension and a public hospitality toward families with different religious backgrounds. Taken together, the public materials demonstrate substantial alignment with classical education expectations, with a notable but minority area of nuance around explicit Socratic seminars in upper grades. Overall classification: CLASSICAL, with a specific area identified as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL (Pedagogy) where more explicit Socratic/seminar-based practices could be foregrounded.

Key signals include:
- Explicit mission emphasizing truth, goodness, beauty, virtue, and wisdom, plus a Great Conversation in Scripture and Great Literature (evidence of religious-metaphysical foundations and pursuit of the true, good, beautiful) ; .
- Orthodox Christian catechesis and optional Christian studies, indicating a grounded theological framework and liturgical/community orientation (liturgical-style practice is suggested by communal singing/ritual life; catechesis is explicit) ; .
- Curriculum elements such as living books, self-assessment/narration, memorization, a liberal-arts framework (grammar/logic/rhetoric), Latin, and the Great Conversation, supporting a Classical-Curriculum orientation ; ; ; .
- Pedagogical emphasis on scholé, contemplative/restful learning, and memory-based practices in lower grades, with upper grades incorporating seminars, rhetoric, and public speaking opportunities (partial Socratic/sem-based practices) ; ; .
- Parents and community partnership is foregrounded, including parent education classes and volunteer involvement as a core part of the school model .

Evidence
- The school foregrounds contemplative, restful learning, and Schole-based pedagogy as a structural emphasis, including a one-room/mastery-based progression in some settings (Charlotte Mason influence) with a focus on "singing," memorization, and memory-based instruction in lower grades ; .

- There is explicit emphasis on upper-school rhetoric and public speech opportunities, suggesting some classical training in rhetoric and oration, though not described as a full, universal Socratic seminar program across all grades ; .
- NoTech classrooms and a discipline-oriented, contemplative approach is described, which aligns with classical prudence and retrenchment from aggressive constructivist/experiential extremes (the lexicon notes negative signals for experiential/project-based learning) ; .

Positive/Negative Keyword Analysis
- Positive indicators present: scholé, contemplation, wonder, memory work, narration, recitation, chanting, liturgical calendar integration, rhetoric, oratory, debate, Paideia-style seminars (as terms appear in the lexicon and in program descriptions).
- Negative indicators: there is limited explicit evidence of fully developed Socratic seminars in the public materials for every grade level, and a broader absence of a uniformly fixed, high-profile "Socratic seminar" across the entire upper-school program; the materials instead emphasize memory/narration and occasional seminars, which is a partial alignment.

Why this rating
- The pedagogy shows robust classical elements (memory/narration, contemplative Scholé, chant, liturgical/calendar integration, rhetoric training) but does not consistently demonstrate full, uniform Socratic seminars or Paideia-style seminars across all high-school levels as an explicit, primary method. What Would Move the Needle

- Pedagogy (Element 4): Introduce more explicit, widely implemented Socratic/seminar-based instruction across upper and possibly middle grades to align with classic Socratic pedagogy expectations (e.g., Paideia-style seminars, dialectic tutorials) in addition to narration and memory work. This would reduce the SMALL gap between current practices (narration, contemplative methods) and a fuller Socratic/seminar-based pedagogy core to classical education. Consider formalizing a K–12 seminary progression, including frequent, teacher-facilitated Socratic seminars, debates, and oral-exam demonstrations, with assessment anchored in the Great Conversation and classical rhetoric. Evidence for this potential improvement could be drawn from the lexicon’s emphasis on Socratic texts and dialectic methods as classical signals and the current limited explicit Socratic use in the public materials. 
- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations (Element 1): Expand explicit liturgical formats or calendar-integrated communal worship or prayer across more grade levels, while maintaining openness to non-religious families. Public materials already reflect Christian formation and catechesis; expanding liturgical integration could further strengthen the liturgical signal without compromising hospitable non-religious access. 
- Curriculum (Element 3): Continue to foreground Great Books/primary sources and ensure a consistent integration of Great Books across core subjects, reinforcing the Great Conversation across the curriculum. While there is evidence of Great Conversation and living books, a more explicit, curriculum-wide Great Books thread across all grades would further strengthen the Classical signal. 
- Faculty development (Element 5): Develop a formalized “portrait of the teacher” and a more explicit “living-curriculum” framework with faculty professional development aligned to classical education benchmarks; this would help ensure consistency of virtuous-model status across all faculty and strengthen the CLASSICAL signal.
