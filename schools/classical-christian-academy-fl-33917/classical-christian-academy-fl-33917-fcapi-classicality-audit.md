## Executive Summary

Classical Christian Academy Florida 33917 (CCA FL 33917) presents a robust classical Christian orientation with clear commitments to a Biblical worldview, a Trivium-inspired curriculum (grammar, logic, rhetoric), and a community framework that partners with families. Public-facing materials emphasize a hybrid model, a classical curriculum, and a faith-centered mission, including explicit statements about forming students as committed disciples of Jesus Christ. Evidence from the school’s materials shows alignment with several core elements of classical education (biblical/theological grounding, formation of character, and a classical pedagogy anchored in the Trivium). However, explicit, sustained public emphasis on some hallmark classical practices (notably sustained Great Books/Great Conversation engagement, Socratic pedagogy across grades 9–12, and a liturgical rhythm as an integral daily practice) is less consistently visible in the available materials. Taken together, the school clearly leans CLASSICAL in several essential dimensions, but several category indicators suggest it falls into SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall rather than fully CLASSICAL.

Overall ranking: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Key insights
- Strong religious/metaphysical grounding evidenced by Biblical worldview, covenantal language, and Christian formation as core aims (Element 1: Classical; evidence includes explicit statements about biblical worldview and Jesus-centered formation).  
- Purpose/Goal reflects formation and virtue-oriented aims consistent with paideia, but public emphasis on the explicit transcendental triad (truth, goodness, beauty) and teleology is more implicit than explicit (Element 2: Somewhat Classical). See mission statements and descriptions of formation. 
- Curriculum shows strong classical markers (grammar, logic, rhetoric; Latin; staged progression) but less explicit public emphasis on Great Books/Great Conversation in the accessible materials (Element 3: Somewhat Classical). 
- Pedagogy includes elements typical of a classical program (grammar and memory-oriented practices in early grades; some exposure to Socratic-style inquiry in upper grades is not clearly evidenced in the accessible content) (Element 4: Somewhat Classical). 
- Faculty materials highlight long-tenured, credentialed teachers and a “living curriculum” approach, signaling a strong classical-influenced faculty culture (Element 5: Classical). 
- Parents & Community are actively engaged as co-educators, with explicit language about partnership and parent involvement (Element 6: Classical).

What would move the needle

- Increase explicit, public emphasis on Great Books and Great Conversation as central components of the curriculum (explicit listing of canonical texts and weekly or semester-long Great Books study across grades, with measurable discussion-based assessment).
- Expand explicit utilization of Socratic pedagogy across upper grades (documented practice: regular Socratic seminars, dialectic dialogues, and structured debates as a core pedagogy in 9–12).
- Increase explicit articulation of liturgical or liturgical-inspired communal practices (daily or weekly chapel, sacred music traditions, liturgical calendar integration) as formal, public components of the educational experience.
- Enhance public-facing descriptions of the Great Conversation framework, the Quadrivium elements (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) within the curriculum, and cross-disciplinary integration to demonstrate full alignment with the Seven Liberal Arts.
- Provide clearer public documentation of “living curriculum” through faculty exemplars and teacher-professional development in classical pedagogy, including explicit portrait of teacher as model of virtue in a public-facing format.
