## Executive Summary

Valor North Austin (VNA) presents a partial alignment with classical education, with clear indications of a liberal-arts, humanities-forward orientation (e.g., Latin, history, integrated humanities programs, and a capstone/advisor structure) and explicit reference to a classical testing pathway (Classical Learning Test). However, public materials from the school's North Austin site do not publicly articulate core religious/metaphysical foundations (theology, liturgy, liturgical practice) or explicit assertions about formation of virtue and wisdom as telos, nor do they openly describe Pedagogy elements such as Socratic seminars, scholé days, or a regimented grammar-logic-rhetoric progression. The result is a cautious classification of SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall, with notable strength in curriculum-oriented clues (Latin, humanities, capstone) but lacking explicit, fully developed religious, metaphysical framing and several pedagogy indicators that would push it toward CLASSICAL.

Key strengths observed in public materials:

- Explicit classical framing via CLT10 (Classical Learning Test) and a humanities-forward curriculum (Upper School Humanities, IHP) alongside a dedicated Capstone program and a Latin program. These elements demonstrate a curricular orientation consistent with classical schooling in structure and content (e.g., humanities, integration of classical disciplines) rather than purely secular or contemporary-density approaches. Evidence includes the CLT10 description and Upper School Humanities (IHP) entries.
- Public materials show structured faculty roles (Headmaster, deans, language and humanities teachers, CAPSTONE advisor) and a formal staff directory, suggesting a coherent, mentorship-oriented faculty model typical of classical schools, though without explicit descriptive language about virtue formation.

Caveats that pull the classification toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL:

- No explicit, public articulation of theology, liturgy, worship, or canonical religious communal practices on the observed pages; no explicit statements about virtue formation, telos, or permanent ideas (truth, goodness, beauty) as metaphysical anchors in the public materials. The absence of explicit religious/metaphysical grounding is a barrier to CLASSICAL across Element 1.
- Pedagogy section does not clearly cite Socratic seminars, scholé, or a rigorous, grade-by-grade movement through grammar, logic, rhetoric in the published materials; memory or chant-based pedagogy is not evidenced in the observed public content. This reduces the strength of a full classical pedagogy claim (Element 4).
- While there is strong curricular mention (Latin, history, humanities), the explicit Great Books/Great Conversation framework, grammar-logic-rhetoric progression, and other hallmark classical signals are not overtly published in the publicly accessible pages. This weighs Element 3 toward SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL rather than CLASSICAL.

What Would Move the Needle

- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations (Element 1)
    - Add explicit, public language tying education to transcendentals (truth, goodness, beauty) and theology or liturgical life as a foundational element; include targeted communications about mission-driven formation and moral aims.
    - Include a brief, public outline of the school’s religious or existential commitments (even if ecumenical) to anchor the formation telos.

- School Purpose and Goal (Element 2)
    - Publish a clear telos statement prioritizing virtue and wisdom formation over workforce preparation, with explicit commitments to the true/good/beautiful as real and knowable.
    - Integrate language about scholé, contemplation, and the cultivation of human excellence in public-facing materials.

- Curriculum (Element 3)
    - Publicly articulate a clear Trivium-inspired framework (grammar, logic, rhetoric) and Great Books or Great Conversation components; specify how history appears across disciplines as continuous study rather than as social studies.
    - Explicitly connect existing programs (IHP, CAPSTONE) to a broader classical-curriculum plan and outline how Latin/history/humanities map to the full classical curriculum.

- Pedagogy (Element 4)
    - Introduce and publicly describe a rigorous classical pedagogy (Socratic seminars in upper grades; memory work and recitation; scholé days; disciplined rhetoric and speech work) and offer examples or course descriptions.
    - If not present, pilot and showcase a classical pedagogy in select grades to demonstrate alignment with the rubric.

- Faculty (Element 5)
    - Publish a formal “living curriculum” statement for faculty, including a portrait of the teacher as mentor and virtuous exemplar; highlight professional development in classical pedagogy and ongoing collaborative culture among teachers.

- Parents & Community (Element 6)
    - Expand explicit programs that treat parents as co-teachers (family liturgies, book studies, classical book clubs) and create more visible, structured opportunities for parent formation and involvement beyond tours and handbooks.
