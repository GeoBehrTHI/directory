## Executive Summary

Cornerstone Classical School Co 81621 (Basalt, CO 81621) presents a strongly faith-centered, family-partnered educational community. Public-facing materials repeatedly frame the school as a Christian, classical-leaning program with an emphasis on character formation, biblical worldview, and community partnership. The site highlights daily Bible-prayer-worship routines, a statement of faith, and a leadership emphasis rooted in Christian discipleship, with explicit mentions of partnering with families through structures like a School Advisory Committee (SAC) and opportunities for parent engagement. While the school clearly affirms a Christian, formation-based aim and includes elements commonly associated with classical education (e.g., Latin, apologetics, structured middle-school debate), it relies on a denominational-curriculum approach (notably Bob Jones University Curriculum) rather than a canonical classical framework centered on Great Books, liberal arts grammar-logic-rhetoric progression, or explicit Socratic pedagogy across its K–12 program. On balance, this evaluation rates Cornerstone Classical School as SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL overall, with clear CLASSICAL markers in religious foundations and parent/community engagement, but limited alignment with core classical curriculum-pedagogy signals and Great Books emphasis.

Executive summary — category-by-category snapshot:
- Religious and Metaphysical Foundations: CLASSICAL
- Purpose and Goal: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Curriculum: NOT CLASSICAL
- Pedagogy: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Faculty: SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL
- Parents & Community: CLASSICAL

What would move the needle: Concrete steps to advance toward full CLASSICAL include (1) articulating and adopting a formal Great Books/great-conversation component across grades, (2) signaling and documenting explicit grammar-logic-rhetoric progressions (especially in upper grades) and Socratic/seminar pedagogy, (3) expanding any liturgical/communal practices into a more explicit “scholé” rhythm (restful contemplative learning) while maintaining doctrine-rich identity, and (4) clearly mapping public-facing materials to the “positive keyword” framework in the Six Essential Elements document (e.g., Partnership with Families, Formation of Affections, Cultural Transmission). These changes would align more fully with the classical rubric while preserving the school’s Christian distinctives.

### Element 2: School Purpose and Goal — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- The school’s stated aim includes formation and moral formation through Christian virtues, with an emphasis on leading students to live out a Christian worldview and to be leaders who impact their world. Statements emphasize formation over mere information and a virtue-oriented aim. Example phrases include “spiritually and academically equips, challenges, inspires” and a focus on forming character within a faith context. 
- There is explicit talk of “forming students” and a moral/spiritual formation language (e.g., “history and values of Christian faith,” “Christian worldview” in the Vision). 
- Public materials emphasize partnership with families and community as a formation context (SAC and parent-tour processes). 

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords observed: Partnership with Families (via SAC), Formation of Affections (implied through worship/prayer and moral formation language), Cultural Transmission (Christian heritage and worldview).
- Negative keywords observed: None overtly present in public materials.

Why this rating
- The school clearly frames education as formation with a virtue-centered aim and a Christian worldview, which aligns with “formation” and “truth-goodness-beauty” moral formation. However, explicit articulation of classical aims centered on liberal-arts virtue-maximization (virtue and wisdom as primary outcomes independent of strictly Christian framing, and explicit reference to the classical liberal-arts progression) is less pronounced in these public materials. Therefore, SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL best captures the balance: formation-centered with clear classical-adjacent elements, but not a fully explicit classical framework in purpose language.

What would move this category to CLASSICAL
- Add explicit language that education aims at the cultivation of virtue and wisdom in the classical sense (truth, goodness, beauty) as timeless ends independent of job preparation, with direct references to “formation” as the central goal.
- Include explicit articulation that the mind can know truth, goodness, and beauty in order to inform the curricular aim (the Great Conversation language is present in the classical rubric but not yet echoed in mission/purpose statements).

### Element 4: School Pedagogy or Teaching Methods — SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL

Evidence
- Middle School is described as “the age of debate” with a block schedule across 6–8th grade, emphasizing logic and the whys of knowledge and God’s role in life. This signals a movement toward dialogic and reasoned inquiry. 
- The middle-school note mentions “disicpleship and mentorship from staff” and “serving within the CCS community,” signaling mentorship-based pedagogy with some Socratic flavor (debate and logic-focused inquiry). Upper-level rhetoric training is not explicitly described, but there is an emphasis on discussion and logic in middle school. 
- There is no consistently explicit mention of Socratic seminars or structured rhetoric practice across grades, nor a stated commitment to regular memorization/recitation in lower grades (Scholé). The content emphasizes a faith-based, practical, and disciplinary approach rather than a formal classical pedagogy.

Positive/Negative keyword analysis
- Positive keywords observed: Socratic discussion (implied by “debate” and “logic” in the middle-school description), discussion-based learning, rhetoric training mentioned as an upper-school possibility (though not explicitly described in practice). The language indicates a shift toward inquiry-based pedagogy in at least some grade bands.
- Negative keywords observed: None explicitly named (no declarations of overly rigid, purely job-prep pedagogy; no explicit “Lack of Scholé” wording).

Why this rating
- The presence of a debate-oriented middle school and emphasis on logic suggests a move toward classical-style pedagogy in parts of the program, but the absence of explicit, school-wide implementation of Socratic seminars, rhetoric-centered upper-level instruction, and a clearly stated grammar-logic-rhetoric progression means the overall pedagogy is not fully classical. Hence SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL.

What would move this category toward CLASSICAL
- Implement and publicly document a formal classical pedagogy across all grades, including:
  - A clear grammar-logic-rhetoric progression from K–12.
  - Regular Socratic seminars, debates, and rhetorical exercises in upper grades.
  - Explicit memorization and scholé routines (restful, contemplative study) embedded into daily/weekly schedules.
  - A plan showing how language arts, history, and literature center around canonical or Great Books selections and guided discussion.

## What Would Move the Needle

- If the school advances toward a fully CLASSICAL profile (moving from SOMEWHAT CLASSICAL in multiple categories), it should consider:
  - Implementing a formal Great Books or canonical reading program with a documented Great Conversation framework, and making explicit how Great Books concepts are introduced and discussed at various grade levels.
  - Establishing and publicizing a grammar-logic-rhetoric progression across K–12, with explicit memorization/recitation practices (Scholé) in lower grades and Socratic seminars in upper grades.
  - Building a public-facing “living curriculum” narrative for Faculty (Element 5)—a formal portrait of teachers as the living curriculum and a collaborative faculty culture anchored in classical pedagogy.
  - Extending the pedagogy to explicitly demonstrate Socratic dialogue, seminars, or debates as standard practice in more grade levels (beyond the stated middle-school emphasis) and articulating a plan for rhetoric training in upper grades.
